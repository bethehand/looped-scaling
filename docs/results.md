# Results summary (after completion of the main grid, 2026-10-07)

> **About this document.** English translation, made on 2026-10-11, of the running results summary kept in Chinese
> since 2026-10-07 (`05_结果汇总.md` in the repository history). Sections 1–4 hold the pre-registered analysis and
> are final; section 5 holds exploratory and extension analyses that are updated as runs finish. File paths are
> relative to `code/` unless they start with `code/` or `docs/`. Figures are in `code/results/figures/`.

This file summarizes all results of the 111 training runs of the main grid, the verdicts on H1 to H4 under the pre-registered rules, the robustness checks, and supplementary analyses that are clearly marked as **exploratory**. The numbers come from `code/results/all_results.csv` and `code/results/fit/`. All fits were done on a Mac in one and the same environment (Python 3.11, numpy 2.4.6, scipy 1.17.1, pandas 3.0.6), with the settings as pre-registered: Huber loss in log space (δ = 10⁻³), L-BFGS-B with 500 starting points, block bootstrap with 1000 resamples.

## 1. Data

- All 111 training runs completed, with 0 failures: 33 each at 10M, 20M and 40M (11 settings × 3 seeds), 11 at 80M, and 1 dense-model run at 160M.
- 474 end-of-cooldown results. Of these, 4 were voided under the collapse rule of 2026-09-30 (3 after the collapse of 40M whole stack r=8 truncated, seed 43; 1 after the severe loss spike of 40M whole stack r=8 full backprop, seed 44).
- 30 training runs had loss spikes (a rise of more than 1.0 nats): middle block r=4 truncated 9 runs, whole stack r=4 truncated 8, whole stack r=8 truncated 8, whole stack r=8 full backprop 5; the dense model, r=2 and the middle-block full-backprop settings had none at all. There was 1 complete collapse. The statistics table is in `code/results/spikes.csv`.
- All 5 pre-run checks of Section 5 of the pre-registration passed (the 40M part of check 2 passed on 10-07).

## 4. Verdicts on H1 to H4 under the pre-registered rules

**H1 (truncated backprop lowers φ; requires φ_trunc < φ_full with non-overlapping intervals on both the iso-token and the iso-FLOP basis): not supported.**
Iso-token basis: middle block φ_trunc 0.00 [0.00, 0.17] vs φ_full 0.13 [0.02, 0.27], in the hypothesized direction but with overlapping intervals; whole stack φ_trunc 0.17 [0.10, 0.20] vs φ_full 0.00 [0.00, 0.16], in the opposite direction, and the intervals again overlap. The effect sizes (0.13 to 0.17) also do not exceed the 20M seed dispersion (up to 0.21). For the iso-FLOP basis, see the supplement at the end of this section.

**H2 (the middle block has a higher φ than the whole stack; raw-loss pairs with similar executed depth are reported alongside): depends on the measure.**
φ basis: with full backprop, middle block 0.13 vs whole stack 0.00 (middle block higher, intervals overlap); with truncation, middle block 0.00 vs whole stack 0.17 (whole stack higher). Raw-loss basis: for the two pairs with similar executed depth (middle block r=8 vs whole stack r=4; middle block r=4 vs whole stack r=2), across 4 rungs × 3 budgets, 24 comparisons in total, the middle block is better in **all** of them, by 0.03 to 0.19 nats, and the gap shrinks as scale grows (−0.19 and −0.15 at 10M 10N, −0.06 and −0.05 at 80M 40N). Under the pre-registered rules this is reported as it stands, as "depends on the measure": the φ comparison is dominated by a single strong cell, whole stack r=8 truncated, whereas the raw loss consistently favors the middle block.

**H3 (φ saturates with r, and the geometric-saturation form beats r^φ; requires lower leave-one-rung-out extrapolation error in both directions and ΔAIC > 10): not supported; the direction is reversed.**
F1 (r^φ) is more accurate in both directions, holding out 80M (0.0084 vs 0.0089) and holding out 10M (0.0194 vs 0.0201), and its AIC is also better by 16 (−6755 vs −6739). Under all 8 robustness variants F1 is no worse than F2. Note that the exploratory analysis in Section 5 shows that within a fixed rung φ does decrease with r (saturation exists), but this is masked by the change of φ with scale, so the pooled fit favors r^φ instead.

**H4 (with matched training FLOPs the optimal r ≤ 2; with matched deployment FLOPs the optimal r is larger): the first half holds but is trivial; the second half is not supported.**
Computed with the fitted formula (`code/fit/h4_optimal_r.py`): at every training-FLOP budget from 10¹⁷ to 10²⁰ the optimum is the dense model (r = 1); within the looped settings, the best is the smallest available r (r=2 with full backprop, r=4 with truncation), which is 0.00 to 0.17 nats worse than the dense model, with the gap shrinking as the budget grows. With matched deployment FLOPs (model size interpolated continuously, training tokens set to 20 times the executed parameter count, or the infinite-data limit), the dense model is again optimal. The joint-fit parameters give the same ranking. The reason: looping saves parameters and GPU memory, not compute; r loops cost about r times as much compute and buy back only r^φ times as many effective parameters, and φ is far below 1.

**H1 on the iso-FLOP basis (supplement, recomputed after correcting the data selection, `code/results/fit/iso_flop/`):** truncated cells enter at their iso-FLOP budgets (more tokens); for full-backprop cells the budgets are the same on both bases. F1: middle block φ_trunc 0.08 [0.00, 0.19] vs φ_full 0.13 [0.02, 0.27], in the hypothesized direction but with overlapping intervals; whole stack φ_trunc 0.16 [0.13, 0.20] vs φ_full 0.00 [0.00, 0.24], in the opposite direction, with overlapping intervals. F2 is the same (0.07 vs 0.14; 0.23 vs 0.00). The two bases agree: **H1 is not supported**. On the iso-FLOP basis the φ of the truncated cells is slightly higher than on the iso-token basis (middle block 0.00 → 0.08), because the extra training tokens offset part of the loss caused by truncation, but this does not change the conclusion.

## 5. Exploratory analyses (not pre-registered, clearly marked)

**5.1 φ as a function of model size (ruler fixed to the main-analysis ruler; φ < 0 is allowed, to express "worse than the iso-parameter dense model"):**

| Cell | 10M | 20M | 40M | 80M |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Middle block / full backprop | −0.17 | 0.15 | 0.30 | 0.38 |
| Middle block / truncated | −0.16 | −0.06 | 0.08 | 0.19 |
| Whole stack / full backprop | −0.41 | −0.22 | −0.14 | 0.04 |
| Whole stack / truncated | 0.23 | 0.18 | 0.15 | 0.09 |

The middle-block φ rises monotonically with scale, from negative values at 10M to 0.38 at 80M; whole stack with full backprop also rises, but only gets close to 0 at 80M; whole stack truncated does the opposite and falls with scale. The pre-registered "constant φ" model cannot accommodate this pattern; this is the root cause of the small φ and the very wide intervals in the main analysis.

**5.2 φ as a function of r (rungs pooled):** middle block full backprop r=2 −0.14, r=4 0.16, r=8 0.17; whole stack full backprop r=2 −0.70, r=4 −0.37, r=8 0.29; whole stack truncated r=4 0.07, r=8 0.20; middle block truncated r=4 −0.17, r=8 0.12. Whole-stack looping gives a positive gain only at r=8. Splitting r within each rung: at 80M, middle block full backprop has φ of 0.58 / 0.52 / 0.32 for r=2 / 4 / 8, i.e. within a fixed rung φ decreases with r (saturation). This does not contradict the pooled H3 verdict: the pooled fit is dominated by the scale effect.

**5.3 Truncation is actually better early in training:** for 20M whole stack r=4, the truncated version gives 3.645 vs 3.789 with full backprop (10N); at 10M it is 3.898 vs 4.135 at 10N, and by 40N the two are equal (3.588); 80M whole stack r=8 gives 3.166 vs 3.224 at 10N, the gap narrows to 3.039 vs 3.052 at 20N, and at 40N the order flips (full backprop 2.907 vs 2.927). Truncation is ahead early on and is caught up with or overtaken once there is more data; for the middle block, truncated and full backprop almost coincide. The 400-step diagnostics with seeds 43 and 44 confirm that this is a reproducible phenomenon.

**5.4 Stability:** loss spikes concentrate in the truncated settings (most at r=4 k=2) and the whole-stack settings, and get worse with scale (80M whole stack r=8 truncated: 13 spikes); the only collapse occurred in 40M whole stack r=8 truncated; one seed of 40M whole stack r=8 full backprop had a 4.27 → 7.87 spike at step 810 and took about 4000 steps to recover, and its results are clearly off. 3 small exploratory experiments with a halved learning rate (10M whole stack r=4 truncated, seeds 42–44, completed 2026-10-08): **none of the three seeds had a single loss spike** (4, 1 and 10 spikes at the original learning rate), and the largest single-step rise was 0.25–0.93. Results (three-seed mean, halved vs original learning rate): 10N 3.942 vs 3.898 (worse by 0.044); 14.4N 3.784 vs 3.798; 20N 3.687 vs 3.703; 28.9N 3.593 vs 3.628; 40N 3.524 vs 3.588 (better by 0.064); 57.8N 3.485 vs 3.519. That is, with the halved learning rate the results are slightly worse early on, and the larger the budget, the better. Conclusion: the spikes are indeed caused by the learning rate; but the improvement at larger budgets cannot be credited entirely to removing the spikes, because longer training by itself also favors a lower constant learning rate (the learning rate was chosen at 10N). This experiment has no halved-learning-rate control for the dense model, so the two cannot be separated, and it is reported only as an exploratory observation.

**5.5 φ is higher on the out-of-distribution validation set:** on the second validation set (SlimPajama), φ of the four cells is 0.31 / 0.18 / 0.02 / 0.30, clearly higher than on the main validation set, which suggests that part of the looped models' gain shows up as out-of-distribution generalization.

Two things hold at the same time. First, the ordering main validation set < second validation set < math < code is consistent across all r=8 settings and all rungs: the gain from looping on math is about 2 to 4 times that on the main validation set, and on code about 4 to 8 times; moreover, at 10M looping gives almost no gain on the main validation set, yet on code it is already better by 0.19 to 0.38. Second, the seed dispersion of the dense model on the out-of-distribution validation sets is far larger than on the main validation set: at 40N the standard deviation across the three dense-model seeds is 0.002 to 0.007 on the main validation set, 0.006 to 0.034 on the second validation set, 0.02 to 0.11 on math and 0.11 to 0.27 on code (the code losses of the three seeds of the 40M dense model are 3.269, 2.728 and 2.946). The training data is FineWeb-Edu web text, so math and code are out of distribution for the models, and the two explanations "looping helps tokens that need multi-step computation" and "looping improves out-of-distribution generalization" cannot be told apart on these data. Refitting φ on the FineMath set under the pre-registered protocol (`code/results/fit/finemath/`) gives 0.49 / 0.33 / 0.43 / 0.63 (middle block full backprop / middle block truncated / whole stack full backprop / whole stack truncated, F1), clearly higher than the 0.13 / 0.00 / 0.00 / 0.17 on the main validation set; on the code set the ruler fit degenerates (α = 1.28), so φ cannot be used.

**5.7 Per-token analysis of which tokens looping helps (exploratory, `code/fit/token_analysis.py`, `code/results/token_analysis/`):** using the 40N-budget checkpoints from before the cooldown (the dense and looped models have seen exactly the same number of tokens), we compare token by token on 2 million tokens of each of the four validation sets. To judge how much of the difference is noise, we use as a null baseline the three dense-model seeds compared against one another (any two equally good models will also each win on some tokens and lose on others), and we average the per-token losses over the three seeds before comparing. Results:
- **On the main validation set, the gain from looping is a diffuse, small downward shift across the board, not concentrated on particular tokens.** After averaging over the three seeds, 20M middle block r=8 is better by 0.012 on average and 40M by 0.025; the looped model is better on only 51% of tokens (seed-vs-seed null baseline 48%–49%); the 10% of tokens with the largest gains account for 55% of the total gain, the same as the baseline (56%–57%). Split into ten difficulty bins, the gain from looping is positive in every bin and leans slightly toward medium difficulty; it is not concentrated on the hardest tokens. The "large gains on hard tokens" seen when comparing single seeds directly is a regression-to-the-mean artifact of the binning, and it appears in the baseline as well.
- **On the out-of-distribution validation sets, the per-token comparison is swamped by seed noise.** On the code set, the mean gap between pairs of dense models swings between −0.47 and +0.79, the same order of magnitude as the looped-vs-dense gap (+0.37 at 40M), so the earlier single-seed observation that "code indentation tokens gain the most" does not hold and is withdrawn; the same goes for the FineMath set (40M +0.11 vs baseline −0.06 to +0.11).
- **Changing the loop count at inference (only the same model is compared, so the conclusion is not affected by noise):** models trained with a fixed loop count only work at the training loop count. When 80M middle block r=8 loops 16 times at inference, its loss on the main validation set rises from 3.08 to 5.21; with 4, 2 and 1 loops it is 4.11, 5.20 and 6.25 respectively; this is consistent across all models and all four validation sets. The whole stack r=8 trained with truncation is insensitive to the loop count: 3.32, 3.15 and 3.18 with 4, 8 and 16 loops, but extra loops bring no gain either. That is, **looped models trained with a fixed loop count do not have the ability to "think a few more times at inference"**; truncation makes them robust to the loop count, but they do not benefit from it. Huginn-style inference-time scaling requires sampling the loop count at random during training (for the results of extension experiment B, see 5.9: it bought robustness, but no gain).
- Interpretation: from 10M to 80M, on web text, looping looks more like "an overall refinement that computes every token a few more times" than something that acts only on the few tokens that need multi-step reasoning; this agrees with the assessment in our internal notes of September 23.

**5.8 Per-loop probes: what the looped block does in each loop (exploratory extension, `code/fit/loop_probe.py`, `code/results/loop_probe/`, Figure 8, Figure 9):** we take the 40N branch-point checkpoints (the same as in 5.7) of 20M/40M/80M middle block r=8 and of 80M whole stack r=8 truncated (k=4), run the looped block for 16 consecutive loops (8 in training), and after each loop read the state in three ways: (a) frozen read-out (logit lens): feed it unchanged into the second half of the model (the last two layers, normalization, output layer), which is the same as looping only that many times at inference; (b) trained adapter: insert a trained d×d linear map (initialized to the identity matrix) before the second half, which tests whether the information is still there and only the coordinate system is wrong; (c) trained linear head: train a linear output head directly on the state. The probes are trained on 10 million tokens of a training stream that the checkpoint has not seen and evaluated on 2 million tokens of the main validation set; we also record the cosine and the relative change between the states of adjacent loops, and the loop at which each token's prediction settles. All numbers are in `summary.csv` and the individual json files.
- **The information is in place within the first few loops; the output layer only understands the coordinate system of the final loop.** With the trained adapter, the state after loop 4 reads out at 3.52 for 20M, 3.42 for 40M and 3.26 for 80M (final loop 8: 3.43 / 3.24 / 3.06), and loops 5 to 7 barely improve any further (80M: 3.21, 3.19, 3.20); with the frozen read-out, loop 4 is worse by 0.28 / 0.92 / 1.03 nats. The information gain readable from loop 1 to loop 8 grows with scale: 20M 0.45, 40M 0.65, 80M 0.71 nats.
- **The final loop is a write-out ("turn"), not one more approximation step.** The cosine between adjacent loop states rises gradually to 0.98–0.99 over loops 1 to 7 (the state is converging), then drops to 0.92–0.94 at loop 7→8, with a relative change of 0.78–1.07 (the change is close to the size of the state itself); for 37% to 44% of tokens the prediction settles only at loop 8, and the frozen read-out at loop 7 is actually worse than at loop 6 (the same at all three rungs). That is, the full-backprop fixed-loop-count model has learned "a fixed 8-step program whose last step moves the result to where the output layer can read it"; the looped block is not an operator that improves the answer loop by loop. This explains the depth curves in 5.7: one loop more or one loop fewer both break this program.
- **Beyond the training loop count the information really does degrade, but not nearly as badly as the frozen read-out suggests.** With the trained adapter, loops 9 to 16 are worse than the final loop by +0.03 to +0.18 at 20M, +0.16 to +0.53 at 40M and +0.19 to +0.56 nats at 80M (roughly back to the level of loop 2), with stronger degradation at larger scale; the frozen read-out gives 3.5–4.8 / 3.6–6.8 / 4.0–7.8. So "more loops at inference make it worse" has two causes: the coordinate-system mismatch (the larger part) and drift of the state itself (the smaller part, but real).
- **Truncated training learns a cycle of period k, not convergence.** The state of 80M whole stack r=8 truncated (k=4) returns to almost the same point every 4 loops (cosine with loop 8 of 0.96, 0.99, 0.99 at loops 4, 12, 16), and a large "turn" happens at every loop that is a multiple of 4 (relative change 1.6–2.3, versus 0.4–0.7 at the other loops); the frozen read-out at loops 4/8/12/16 is 3.32/3.15/3.17/3.18, and 4.0–5.4 at the other loops. That is, backpropagating only through the last 4 loops makes the model learn "4 loops" as one unit and execute it repeatedly; executing it twice is 0.17 better than once, and further repetitions bring no gain. This explains why the truncated model in 5.7 is "robust to the loop count, but gains nothing from extra loops": the robustness comes from the periodicity, not from loop-by-loop improvement.
- **Predictions for hard tokens settle later.** Tokens whose final prediction is wrong settle later than those predicted correctly (share settling only at loop 8: 20M 44% vs 26%, 40M 48% vs 36%, 80M 46% vs 34%); across ten difficulty bins, the three easiest bins settle on average at loops 2.4–4.6 and the other seven bins at loops 5.0–5.6 (whole stack truncated: 3.5 vs 6.2). This is top-1 evidence that "hard tokens need more loops", but it must be discounted: the final-loop "turn" flips predictions that were uncertain to begin with, and hard tokens have flatter distributions that are flipped more easily.
- **Seed replication (added 2026-10-10, Figure 8b):** the four checkpoints of seeds 43 and 44 of 20M and 40M middle block r=8 all reproduce "loop 7 worse than loop 6, sharp drop at loop 8" and "with the trained adapter, loop 4 is already close to the final value" (e.g. 40M seed 43, frozen read-out 3.71 → 3.86 → 3.27; seed 44, 4.05 → 4.21 → 3.27); whole stack r=8 truncated is a clean period-4 cycle for both seeds at 40M (42, 44) (frozen read-out at loops 4/8/12/16 of 3.41/3.27/3.27/3.28 and 3.45/3.35/3.40/3.49, 3.9–5.1 at the other loops), and 20M seed 42 is approximately period 4 but drifts slightly (minima at loops 4, 8, 12, 15). The tokens that settle later are still those predicted wrongly (consistent across seven checkpoints).
- Interpretation and caveats: (1) the main conclusions agree across the three rungs (1–3 seeds per rung), and the periodic behavior of the truncated whole stack is an additional finding; (2) the linear-head probe is under-trained (10 million tokens; initialized from the output layer, but its input is not the kind of feature the output layer is used to), so its absolute values are only upper bounds; the whole-stack model has no tail layers, and there the linear head and the frozen read-out are close, which serves as a check; (3) the probes only show "what is in the state", not whether the model is "reasoning"; (4) implications for latent-space reasoning: the fixed-loop-count looped models from 10M to 80M have no mechanism by which "thinking a few more loops makes it better"; what they implement is a fixed-length (or fixed-period) program; whether random loop-count training (extension experiment B) can induce a loop-by-loop improvement mechanism is the question to be tested directly next.

**5.9 Random loop-count training (extension experiment B, `configs/ext/*rs1to8*`, `results/token_analysis/depth_curve_randr.csv`, Figure 10):** Huginn-style recipe: 20M middle block r=8, with a loop count drawn uniformly from 1 to 8 at each optimizer step (4.5 loops on average; training FLOPs about 56% of fixed 8 loops), evaluated at the nominal 8 loops; one run each with full backprop and with truncation (k=4, backprop only through the last min(4, r) loops actually run), seed 42, budgets 10N/20N/40N. End-of-cooldown loss on the main validation set, compared with the main-grid controls of the same seed:

| Budget | Random count | Random count, truncated | Fixed 8 loops | Fixed 8 loops, truncated | Fixed 4 loops | Dense model |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10N | 3.728 | 3.722 | 3.666 | 3.665 | 3.660 | 3.655 |
| 20N | 3.499 | 3.493 | 3.446 | 3.449 | 3.443 | 3.468 |
| 40N | 3.340 | 3.336 | 3.289 | 3.299 | 3.295 | 3.340 |

- **Fully robust to the loop count, but extra loops bring no gain.** Inference loop-count curve of the 32N trunk checkpoint on 2 million tokens of the main validation set: 1 loop 3.573, 2 loops 3.519, 3 loops 3.508, 4 loops 3.505, 5 loops 3.504, and from there up to 32 loops unchanged to four decimal places (3.5038–3.5039); the truncated version behaves the same (3.566 → 3.496); the second validation set, math and code agree, so all four validation sets are consistent (on the code set, going from 8 to 32 loops lowers the loss by a further 0.002, while the truncated version instead rises by 0.009; both negligible). By contrast, the fixed-8-loop control only works at loop 8 (3.453), with 3.5 to 4.7 at the other loop counts (Figure 10).
- **Mechanism: the looped block has become a contraction.** Per-loop probes (same method as 5.8) show a cosine between adjacent loop states of 0.95 (1→2), 0.99 (3→4) and 1.000 (from loop 6 on), with the relative change falling from 0.34 to 0.008; the frozen read-out and the trained adapter differ by only 0.03 (no coordinate-system mismatch); for 76% of tokens the prediction no longer changes from loop 1 on, and predictions settle after 1.5 loops on average (1.7 loops in the hardest bin). What it has learned is "iterate to a fixed point": it converges in about 4 loops, and after that any number of loops gives the same result.
- **The price is that the entire advantage of looping is wiped out.** At the nominal 8 loops it is 0.05 nats worse than fixed 8 loops (40N: 3.340 vs 3.289), exactly tied with the dense model (3.340 vs 3.340), and also worse than fixed 4 loops, which uses similar training compute (3.295). So at 20M, on web text, random loop-count training only buys robustness to the loop count, not a gain from extra loops at inference.
- **Read together with 5.8.** Fixed-loop-count model = "about 4 loops of approximation + a nonlinear turn in the final loop", with the turn worth 0.07–0.1 nats (which the trained adapter cannot recover: loop 7 3.53 vs loop 8 3.43); the random-count model keeps only the approximation part, and its converged level (3.50) equals the level of the fixed-count model at loops 5 to 7 with the trained adapter (3.50). Under both training schemes, the extra information the looped block can extract from text saturates at around 4 loops. With random counts, truncation with k=4 makes no difference compared with full backprop (≤0.01); the period-4 cycle of 5.8 appears only with a fixed count plus truncation.
- **Implications for latent-space reasoning.** "Computing a few more loops at inference makes it better" did not appear at this scale and on this kind of data. This is not because extra loops break the model (random loop-count training has already removed that), but because no amount of extra looping finds any new information to extract. Seeing a gain requires tasks in which each token really needs many steps of serial computation, which is what extension experiment C (docs/synthetic-reasoning-design.md) is meant to test.
- Caveats: single seed, 20M only, loss only; the random range 1–8 differs from Huginn's distribution (log-normal Poisson, mean 32); the probes and curves use the 32N trunk checkpoint, not the model after cooldown.

- **Relation to the main conclusions.** The pre-registered φ was fitted on 10N–40N; this section shows that φ itself depends on D/N: the same 10M r=8 cell has φ<0 at 10N (worse than the iso-parameter dense model), but at 160N it is equivalent to 1.8 times the data. - Caveats: two seeds (42, 43) for the middle-block cells, seed 42 only for the whole-stack cells; the equivalence between continuing from the 32N checkpoint and training from scratch to 80N/160N is guaranteed by a test (`test_init_from_continues_an_earlier_trunk`, bit-identical), but multiple branches from the same trunk share the randomness of the first 32N and are not independent of one another; the same holds for the 10M dense model at 160N, which continues from the 64N checkpoint.

- **Looping buys steps, but with the same amount of training it falls short of the iso-depth dense model**: at equal parameters, looped 8 loops (5) > dense 8 layers (4); the iso-depth dense 36 layers (4.4 times the parameters) is best (7). In the looped conditions the learning curves were still rising steeply at 16000 steps (8-step accuracy of looped 8 loops at 5e-4 was 0.26/0.42/0.63 at 13000/14000/15000 steps), so the formal runs were changed to 32000 steps.
- **More loops at inference solve longer problems, but this happens for only one training outcome**: the looped 8-loop model with lr 5e-4 scores 0.62, 0.65 and 0.64 on 10-step problems with 12, 16 and 32 loops, and 0.22, 0.36 and 0.36 on 12-step problems, while short problems are unaffected (with 32 loops, 1–8 steps are the same as with 8 loops); the iso-depth dense 36 layers scores only 0.05 and 0.02 on 10 and 12 steps. The same architecture with lr 1e-3 only works at 8 loops (with 4 or 16 loops everything falls to the floor), the same "fixed-length program" behavior as the text models in 5.7 and 5.8. The random loop-count model is robust to the loop count but does not extrapolate (consistent with 5.9); the step-matched model extrapolates to 9–12 steps when loops = d (0.49/0.35/0.19/0.12), and gets worse with more loops than d (there is no fixed point at which it stops).
- **Intermediate-value probes show "one loop, one step" directly** (after each loop, the intermediate value x_j is decoded linearly at the separator of step j): for looped 8 loops at 5e-4, the decodable frontier advances about one step per loop (x₃, x₄, x₅, x₆, x₇, x₈ cross 0.5 at loops 2–3, 3–5, 4–6, 5–7, 6–9 and 7–11 respectively), and values stay unchanged once computed; with 16 loops x₈ reaches 0.68. This is why it can solve longer problems by looping more. The same architecture at lr 1e-3 has no advancing frontier (x₃ is 0.31 at 8 loops); the frontier of the random loop-count model stops after loop 3 (x₄ 0.30, x₅ 0.17); the frontier of the step-matched model advances one step per loop, but after loop 9 values that have already been computed start to be destroyed (x₄ drops from 0.77 to 0.45). The dense 36 layers barely compute the chain in the first 16–20 layers (x₂ is only 0.52 at layer 20) and after that advance about one step every 2 layers; the dense 8 layers reach x₄ in the last 4 layers.
- Interpretation (to be tested by the formal runs): a loop in the architecture **can** be learned as a step-by-step reasoning algorithm, and one with "depth on demand": given more loops at inference, it solves longer problems; but whether it is learned depends on the training (learning rate, loop-count schedule), and the same architecture can instead learn a fixed-length program. The formal suite runs under the protocol fixed in docs/deviation-log.md on 2026-10-11 (learning-rate sweep → selection → seeds 2, 3 × 32000 steps → probes) and started on October 11 at 03:20.

**5.14 Text models: the learning rate decides whether the looped block learns a "fixed program" or a "convergent iteration" (exploratory, existing checkpoints, `results/token_analysis_looplr/depth_curve.csv`):** prompted by 5.13, we take the checkpoints of the 20M loop learning-rate check of September 25 (learning rates of 0.5×, 0.71× and 1.0×; 8N trunk checkpoints of the 10N budget; seed 42) and measure the loss at inference loop counts 1–16 on 1 million tokens of the main validation set. Middle block r=8: at 0.5× the loss at 4–16 loops lies within 3.902–3.920 (almost flat), at 1.0× within 3.877–3.955 (8 loops clearly best; 7 and 9 loops worse by 0.07–0.08); middle block r=4: the range over 4–16 loops is 0.105 at 0.5× and 0.257 at 1.0×. That is, **the lower the learning rate, the closer to a convergent iteration; the higher, the more specialized to the training loop count**, in the same direction as 5.13; but the price is the loss at the nominal loop count (middle block r=8 at 8 loops: 0.5× 3.902, 1.0× 3.877, which is exactly why the September learning-rate check chose 1.0×). Whatever the learning rate, more loops than in training bring no gain on text (at 0.5×, 12 and 16 loops give 3.905 and 3.907 vs 3.902 at 8 loops). The whole-stack cells are highly specialized at all learning rates (whole stack r=4: 6.6–9.2 at the other loop counts), and truncated whole stack r=8 is a period-4 cycle at all learning rates. Caveat: these checkpoints were trained only to 8N; on the 32N checkpoints of 5.8 the "final-loop turn" is stronger (20M middle block r=8: 7 loops vs 8 loops differ by 0.55); single seed.

## 6. Limitations

- The ruler parameters are weakly identified (E at the lower bound, α = 0.054), so the absolute value of φ depends on the fitting method; both fits are reported.
- The constant-φ model is systematically misspecified on these data (Section 5.1); the results of the tests of the pre-registered hypotheses should be interpreted together with the exploratory analyses.
- 80M has only one seed; 160M has only the dense model.
- On the iso-FLOP basis, the truncated cells cover only two values of r, r=4 and r=8.
- The FineMath and code sets are out of distribution relative to the training data; the two explanations "looping helps multi-step computation" and "looping improves out-of-distribution generalization" cannot be distinguished on these data.
- The training instability of the 20M and 40M whole-stack settings (loss spikes and one collapse) increased the variance; the robustness fit with the spiked runs removed gives similar φ.
