\section{What a loop is worth on text}
\label{sec:text}

\subsection{The pre-registered law cannot say what a loop is worth}
\label{sec:verdicts}

The pre-registration asked what a loop is worth through one number per cell, the exponent $\varphi$ of
$\Neff = \Nonce + r^{\varphi}\Nrec$, read off a dense ruler fitted to the 54 dense results of the grid. That ruler puts
its irreducible loss at the lower bound of the fit ($E = 0.050$) and its parameter exponent at $\alpha = 0.054$, far
below the usual value of about 0.3; five sizes cannot pin down $E$, $A$ and $\alpha$ separately. $\varphi$ inherits
the weakness: it is 0--0.17 per cell with the primary fit and 0--0.41 with the joint fit over all 352 results, which
gives $\alpha = 0.267$ (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}). None of the four hypotheses held as stated \lbl{(pre-registered)}.
Truncated backpropagation, predicted to lower $\varphi$ (H1), pointed that way in the middle block, with overlapping
intervals, and the other way in the whole stack, where it also lowered the loss at every size with four loops; it
brought most of the loss spikes as well (Appendix~\ref{app:training}). The full verdicts are in
Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}. What survives is a trend: with the ruler fixed, the middle block looped 4 times has
$\varphi = -0.32$, 0.19, 0.39, 0.52 and 0.65 from 10M to 160M \lbl{(exploratory}; read with the same weak ruler, so
only its direction is used), so a single $\varphi$ per cell averages over very different regimes. We therefore measure the value of a loop without a fitted law.

\subsection{Measured directly, a loop's value rises with model size and data}
\label{sec:multiplier}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig2_multiplier}
  \caption{What each parameter of a model with a looped middle block is worth: the multiplier $M = \Nstar/N$, where
  $\Nstar$ is the size of the dense model that reaches the same loss on the same number of training tokens
  (interpolated between the measured dense sizes, including the dense models continued in S4, Section~\ref{sec:equalcompute};
  open markers: beyond the largest or smallest by at most a factor of two). $M = 1$: as good as a dense model of its own size. Diamonds: dense models with 18 layers and the looped model's
  number of parameters (Section~\ref{sec:depth}). Bars: 95\% intervals from resampling seeds (80M has one seed).}
  \label{fig:multiplier}
\end{figure}

The dense runs themselves can serve as the ruler. For each looped model we read the loss of every measured dense size
at the looped model's number of training tokens and interpolate the dense size $\Nstar$ with the same loss; $M =
\Nstar/N$ is what each parameter of the looped model is worth in dense parameters (Figure~\ref{fig:multiplier},
\lbl{exploratory}). For the middle block looped 4 times, $M$ rises from 0.6--1.0 at 10M--20M with 10--20 tokens per
parameter to 1.26--1.46 at 40M--160M with 20--40 (the 160M values lie beyond the largest dense model, by less than a
factor of two); at 10M with 10 tokens per parameter it cannot be read off, because the looped model is worse than both
dense models measured at that number of tokens. The rise with data is carried by 10M--40M and 160M; at 80M, with one
seed, $M$ is 1.49, 1.40 and 1.45 at $10N$, $20N$ and $40N$, flat in data. $M$ counts every parameter, including the
output head, which the loop does not multiply and which is 34\% of $N$ at 10M and 21\% at 40M; per looped parameter,
the local exponent $\varphi_{\mathrm{loc}}$ is negative where $M < 1$ ($-0.84$ at 20M with 10 tokens per parameter) and
0.22--0.55 from 40M on. A least-squares plane in $\log_2 M$ gives a factor of 1.21 per doubling
of model size and 1.14 per doubling of tokens per parameter (Appendix~\ref{app:fits}). Eight loops add up to 0.16 over
four at 10M--20M and nothing consistent from 40M on. At 40M--160M with 20--40 tokens per parameter, counting $N$ without the
output head as \citet{schwethelm2026how} do, the local exponent is $\varphi_{\mathrm{loc}} = 0.32$--0.50, close to their
$\varphi = 0.46$.

The shared learning rate does not create the trend. Re-tuning the dense model and the middle block looped 4 times at
10M--40M with one seed (two at 20M; supplementary experiment S1, \lbl{pre-specified}; Appendix~\ref{app:lr}) moved the gap between them by at most
0.005 nats at 20M--40M and about 0.02 at 10M, where the looped model preferred lower rates; with each at its best rate,
the size trend at $40N$ is somewhat smaller but holds by the criterion of the plan, and the data trend at 10M is larger
($+0.082$ at $10N$ to $-0.059$ at $160N$). Published equal-parameter results, restated in the same unit, mostly agree in
comparable settings (1.23--1.46 at 134M--770M) and go higher with larger models or far more tokens per parameter
(Appendix~\ref{app:published}); they barely sample models below 40M trained on 20 or fewer tokens per parameter, where we
find the loop worth at most its own parameters. That the value rises with tokens per parameter is a dependence that
effective-parameter laws whose loop gain does not depend on the training tokens \citep{schwethelm2026how,chen2026moe} do
not capture.

Every dense model of this ruler has eight layers, while a middle block looped 4 times executes 20, and at a fixed size
deeper and thinner models do better below a billion parameters \citep{liu2024mobilellm}, though the shape matters little
over a wide range of shapes at a fixed size \citep{kaplan2020scaling}. Is $M$ what sharing buys, or what depth buys at a fixed size?

\subsection{At 40M, a deeper dense model of the same size recovers about half of it}
\label{sec:depth}

Supplementary experiment S10 \lbl{(pre-specified)} trained dense models with 18 layers and the looped model's number of
parameters: $18 \times 320$ ($N = 28.1$M) at 20M and $18 \times 448$ ($N = 51.2$M) at 40M, an executed depth of 18
against the looped model's 20. They use the grid's recipe, data, budgets and three seeds, and the learning rate the
grid's rule gives their width (the hidden layers scaled by $(18/12)^{-1/2}$), called the rule's rate below. Their
multiplier $M_{\mathrm{deep}}$ is read off the same 8-layer dense runs. Its plan fixed the reading in advance: $R = \log M_{\mathrm{deep}} / \log
M_{\mathrm{loop}}$ at 40M, averaged over the three budgets, is the share of the looped model's advantage that a dense
model of the same size and depth recovers.

At 40M the deep dense model is worth 1.10, 1.11 and 1.11 dense parameters per parameter at $10N$, $20N$ and $40N$,
against 1.15, 1.26 and 1.34 for the looped model (Figure~\ref{fig:multiplier}a, diamonds). $R$ is 0.50 (95\% interval
0.41--0.62, resampling seeds), in the band that the plan reads as ``partly depth, partly sharing'', as it predicted. At 40M,
what depth buys does not grow with data; the looped model's lead over the deep dense model does, from 0.003 nats at $10N$ to
0.015 and 0.029 at $20N$ and $40N$, with the same sign in all three seeds at the two longer budgets. At 20M the deep model,
narrow at width 320, is worse than the 8-layer one of the same size ($M_{\mathrm{deep}} = 0.84$--0.94), so there depth
explains none of the looped model's advantage at $40N$ ($M_{\mathrm{loop}} = 1.16$; the plan reports 20M descriptively).

The learning rate qualifies the shortest budget. At 40M the deep model was best at 1.41 times the rule's rate, the
highest rate tried (seed 42), at all three budgets, by 0.014, 0.006 and 0.001 nats. With each model at its best tested
rate, as the plan asks in that case, the deep model is ahead of the looped one at $10N$ by 0.010 nats ($M = 1.37$
against 1.18, so $R = 1.91$), and at $20N$ and $40N$ $R$ is 0.56 and 0.34 (0.45 and 0.37 for the same seed at the
rule's rate); since
its best rate lies at the edge of the range tried at all three budgets, and the plan had no rule to extend the range,
these readings may understate the deep model. Averaged over the three budgets, $R$ at the best rates is 0.94, which by
the bands the plan set for the primary reading would read as ``most of the advantage is a depth effect''; the average is carried by $10N$, where $\log M_{\mathrm{loop}}$ is only 0.17 and the ratio is unstable, and
the plan keeps the primary reading at the rule's rate.
The deep model also costs about half the training compute of the looped model (406M against 835M FLOPs per token at
40M). So at 40M about half of what the loop buys at equal parameters is depth, and at the shortest budget, once the deep
model's learning rate is tuned, all of it (one seed, by 0.010 nats); at 20M none of it is; and the part that grows with
data is not recovered by depth (Appendix~\ref{app:s10}). What the loop buys at equal parameters, it pays for in
compute.

\subsection{At equal training compute, the dense model wins all but one measured comparison}
\label{sec:equalcompute}

At equal parameters, a middle block looped 2, 4 or 8 times costs 1.4--1.5, 2.2--2.4 or 3.7--4.1 times the training FLOPs
per token of the dense model. For an equal-compute comparison we give the dense model the tokens it could have seen
with the looped run's compute and read its loss off the measured dense curve \lbl{(exploratory)}. Of the 320 looped
results of the grid and its extensions, 221 could be compared this way with the original dense runs, and the dense model
won all of them, by at least 0.016 nats; the other 99 needed the dense loss beyond its measured curve, which two
extrapolations bracket (Appendix~\ref{app:lr}), and with the one that flatters looping, looping came out ahead in 6 of
them, by at most 0.021 nats, all at the largest data per parameter.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig3_equal_compute}
  \caption{Equal training compute: looped minus dense loss when the dense model of the same size is trained on the
  tokens the looped run's compute would buy (positive: dense better). Filled markers: dense loss measured, including the
  dense models continued to $640N$ (10M) and $320N$ (20M) in S4. Open markers with bars: the dense loss is extrapolated;
  the bar spans the two bounds (power law to last segment). Diamonds: the 10M model looped 4 times trained at 0.35
  times the dense rate, the lowest rate S1 tried (mean of two seeds; bars: seed range).}
  \label{fig:equalcompute}
\end{figure}

Continuing the 10M dense models to $640N$ and the 20M ones to $320N$ (supplementary experiment S4, \lbl{pre-specified})
turned 37 of the extrapolated comparisons into measured ones (Figure~\ref{fig:equalcompute}). At the shared learning
rate the dense model wins all 258 measured comparisons, which share dense curves, trunks and seeds and are not
independent, and the measured gaps fall about halfway between the two extrapolations. The margin shrinks with data per
parameter: for the 10M middle block looped 4 times it is $+0.119$ at $40N$ (one seed's dense loss extrapolated),
$+0.062$ at $80N$ and $+0.016$ at $160N$ (Figure~\ref{fig:equalcompute}; Table~\ref{tab:equalcompute} in
Appendix~\ref{app:lr} gives the largest budgets). With each model
at its best tested learning rate (S1), the dense model won 10 of the 11 comparisons (one of them, 40M at $40N$, on an
extrapolated dense loss); the eleventh, at 10M and $160N$ with
the looped model at 0.35 times the dense rate, the lowest rate tried, where its loss still fell as the rate was lowered,
is level on average ($-0.011$ and $+0.005$ in two seeds): the only measured equal-compute comparison in this study that
the dense model does not win. The deep dense models of Section~\ref{sec:depth}, at about half the looped model's compute
per token, are ahead by 0.11--0.19 nats where their curves reach without extrapolation \lbl{(exploratory)}. The other 62
comparisons still need an extrapolated dense loss (Appendix~\ref{app:lr}). Within the measured range, loops trade
compute for parameters and data; they do not save compute.

\subsection{At deployment, the loop saves some weight memory and pays for every executed layer}
\label{sec:cost}

We measured inference on one RTX 4090 in bf16 with KV-cached decoding and one cache per executed layer, the
computation the models were trained with (supplementary experiment S3, \lbl{pre-specified}; Appendix~\ref{app:cost}). At
these sizes decoding time follows the executed layers: at equal parameters, a middle block looped 2, 4 or 8 times takes
1.5, 2.4--2.6 or 4.5--4.6 times the decoding time per token of the dense model, close to the ratios of executed layers.
Against the dense model that reaches the same loss at $40N$, the middle block looped 4 times needs 4\% less weight memory
at 20M, rising to 19\% at 160M (where the dense model of equal loss is extrapolated), but 2.20--2.41 times the KV cache
per token, 2.37--2.47 times the decoding time and about 1.6 times the peak memory at batch 32. Sharing the KV cache across
loops would remove most of the cache and memory cost, though not the decoding time. Applied after training to the 20M trunk checkpoints at $32N$ of one seed (supplementary experiment S6,
\lbl{pre-specified}), it costs every model that gains from its loops 2.2 to 7.4 times that gain (0.09--0.17 nats against
gains of 0.019--0.046 over the dense model at the same checkpoint and on the same tokens; the dense loss was measured after
a review, Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}), so that it would fall below the dense model of its size; by the plan's rule (an increase of at most
0.02 nats: sharing usable; up to 0.1: it degrades; above 0.1: it breaks), sharing degrades the fixed 8-loop model (0.09)
and breaks the truncated one (0.14) and the 4-loop models (0.17; 0.10 at half the learning rate, at the boundary). Only the models trained with a random loop
count, whose block contracts to a fixed point (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}) and which gain nothing over the dense model,
share for free. Training towards convergence, or with a shared cache in the scheme of \citet{monea2026shared}, in which only the
first loop writes a cache and which costs no quality there, may avoid this; we tested neither.

\paragraph{What the loop buys on text.} At these sizes the loop trades compute for parameters and data. From about 40M
on and with 20--40 tokens per parameter, each parameter of a model whose middle block is looped 4 times is worth 1.3--1.5
dense parameters, more with more data; at 40M a dense model of the same size and nearly the same depth gets about half
of that advantage for half the compute, but not the part that grows with data. At equal training compute the dense
model wins every measured comparison but one, and at deployment the loop pays for every executed layer. The second claim, that more loops at inference buy test-time compute,
is the subject of the next two sections.
