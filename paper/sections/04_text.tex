\section{What a loop is worth on text}
\label{sec:text}

\subsection{The pre-registered verdicts}
\label{sec:verdicts}

The dense ruler, fitted to the 54 dense results of the grid, puts its irreducible loss at the lower bound of the fit
($E = 0.050$) and its parameter exponent at $\alpha = 0.054$, far below the usual value of about 0.3; five sizes cannot
pin down $E$, $A$ and $\alpha$ separately. The joint fit over all 352 results gives $\alpha = 0.267$ and larger
$\varphi$. Table~\ref{tab:prereg} gives $\varphi$ per cell and the verdicts \lbl{(pre-registered)}.

Two observations from the grid matter for what follows. First, truncated backpropagation, predicted to hurt, helped
whole-stack loops with four loops at every size (Table~\ref{tab:gaps}): at 20M a whole stack looped 4 times reached 3.646
with truncation and 3.783 with full backpropagation at $10N$ (three-seed means); with eight loops it was slightly worse
at 10M--20M. Separately, at 10M the only looped cells better than the dense model on average over the three budgets
were whole-stack loops with $r = 8$. Second, truncation and whole-stack loops brought instability: 30 of the 111 runs had
at least one loss spike, 25 of them with truncated backpropagation (16 of these whole-stack loops) and the other five
whole-stack loops with $r = 8$; one run collapsed. Halving the learning rate removed the spikes in a 10M follow-up
(Appendix~\ref{app:training}).

\subsection{Why \texorpdfstring{$\varphi$}{phi} is not identified, and what to measure instead}
\label{sec:multiplier}

$\varphi$ converts a loss into effective parameters through the ruler, and the ruler is weakly identified: with the
primary fit $\varphi$ is 0--0.17 per cell, with the joint fit 0--0.41. Its trend across sizes is robust, though. With
the ruler fixed, the middle block looped 4 times has $\varphi = -0.32$, 0.19, 0.39, 0.52 and 0.65 from 10M to 160M
\lbl{(exploratory)}. A single $\varphi$ per cell averages over very different regimes.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig2_multiplier}
  \caption{What each parameter of a model with a looped middle block is worth, measured without a fitted law: the multiplier
  $M = \Nstar/N$, where $\Nstar$ is the size of the dense model that reaches the same loss on the same number of
  training tokens (interpolated between the measured dense sizes, including the dense models continued in S4; open
  markers: beyond the largest or smallest dense size by
  at most a factor of two). $M = 1$: the looped model is as good as a dense model of its own size. Bars: 95\% intervals
  from resampling seeds (80M has one seed).}
  \label{fig:multiplier}
\end{figure}

The dense runs themselves can serve as the ruler. For each looped model we read the loss of every measured dense
size at the looped model's number of training tokens and interpolate the dense size $\Nstar$ with the same loss
(Figure~\ref{fig:multiplier}, \lbl{exploratory}). The multiplier $M = \Nstar/N$, what each parameter of the looped model is worth
in dense parameters, rises for the middle block looped 4 times from 0.6--1.0 at 10--20M with 10--20 tokens per parameter
to 1.26--1.46 at 40M--160M with 20--40; the 160M values lie beyond the largest dense model, by less than a factor of
two. At 10M with 10 tokens per parameter $M$ cannot be read off: the looped model is worse than both dense models
measured at that number of tokens, and those two are out of order (the 20M one, at 5.7 tokens per parameter, is worse
than the 10M one), so $\Nstar$ cannot be extrapolated either. Counting $N$ without the output head, as
\citet{schwethelm2026how} do, the local exponent there is $\varphi_{\mathrm{loc}} = 0.32$--0.50, close to their
$\varphi = 0.46$ (0.35--0.52 with the head). A least-squares
plane in $\log_2 M$ gives a factor of 1.21 per doubling of model size and 1.14 per doubling of tokens per parameter (1.16
with 8 loops), with classical 95\% intervals, which include the plane's misfit, of 1.13--1.30 and 1.05--1.24; by these
intervals the product term is indistinguishable from zero. On the plane the loop breaks even ($M = 1$) at about
$N = 35$M, between the 20M and 40M models, with 20 tokens per parameter, and at about $N = 12$M, below the smallest
model ($N = 15.4$M), with 80. The measured points agree at 80 tokens per parameter (1.04 for the 10M model, 1.05 on the
plane) but reach $M = 1$ earlier at 20: the 20M model ($N = 26.8$M) is already at 0.99, where the plane gives 0.93. Eight loops add up to 0.16 over
four at 10M--20M and nothing consistent from 40M on.

Published equal-parameter results mostly restate to a similar range in comparable settings, and to higher values at
larger sizes or with far more tokens per parameter. Where a paper reports a non-looped ladder, we interpolate it in the
same way. Parcae's models, trained with a loop count sampled around a mean of 8 and backpropagated through 4 of them,
and run with 8, are worth 1.31--1.46 dense parameters per parameter at 140M--770M \citep{prairie2026parcae}, against a
ladder trained on the same tokens per parameter, which understates the value. Ouro's models looped 4 times are worth
1.23 and 1.36 at 134M and 374M (about 150 and 50 tokens per parameter) and more than 1.75 at 778M, beyond its largest
non-looped model, but less than their parameters at 53M despite about 380 tokens per parameter, measured by mean
benchmark accuracy read off a plot (\citealp[App.~D.1]{zhu2025scaling}). The two 12-layer looped models of \citet{saunshi2025reasoning}, about 0.5B
parameters looped 2 and 4 times, are worth 1.26 and 1.36, and their shallower blocks looped 3, 4 and 6 times
1.47--1.95, at about 750--1500 tokens per parameter. The law of \citet{schwethelm2026how} implies 1.25, 1.45 and 1.56
for 2, 4 and 8 loops in their iso-depth layouts. These studies differ from ours in size, data and recipe, so the
agreement is a consistency check, not a pooled estimate. They barely sample models below 40M trained on 20 or fewer
tokens per parameter, where we find a model with a looped middle block worth at most about as many dense
parameters as it has. That the value rises with tokens per
parameter at equal parameters is a dependence that effective-parameter laws whose loop gain depends on the loop
count (and, for mixtures of experts, on the sparsity) but not on the training tokens
\citep{schwethelm2026how,chen2026moe} do not capture; it agrees with the recommendation of \citet{prairie2026parcae} to
increase looping and data in tandem, and with the finding of \citet{chen2026growth} that with repeated data the best
loop count grows with compute.

\subsection{At equal training compute, the dense model wins by less and less}
\label{sec:equalcompute}

At equal parameters, a middle block looped 2, 4 or 8 times costs 1.4--1.5, 2.2--2.4 or 3.7--4.1 times the training FLOPs
per token of the dense model. For an equal-compute comparison we give the dense model the tokens it could have seen
with the looped run's compute and read its loss off the measured dense curve \lbl{(exploratory)}. Of the 320 looped results of the grid and
its extensions, 221 could be compared this way with the original dense runs; the dense model won all of them, by at
least 0.016 nats. The other 99 needed the dense loss beyond its measured curve. Two extrapolations bracket it: in a
backtest that drops the last budgets of every measured dense curve, extending the last segment predicts a dense loss
0.023--0.036 nats too low one doubling out (it flatters the dense model), and a power law fitted to the whole curve one
0.006--0.027 too high (it flatters looping). With the power law, looping came out ahead in 6 of the 99, by at most
0.021 nats, all at the largest data per parameter.

We then continued the 10M dense models to $640N$ and the 20M ones to $320N$ (supplementary experiment S4,
\lbl{pre-specified}), which turns 37 of the extrapolated comparisons into measured ones (Figure~\ref{fig:equalcompute},
Table~\ref{tab:equalcompute}). The dense model wins all 258 measured comparisons, and the measured gaps fall about
halfway between the two extrapolations. The S4 plan had predicted gaps between $-0.01$ and $+0.04$, the dense model
ahead in most and none below $-0.02$; the last two held, but four of the nine measured top-budget gaps exceeded $+0.04$
(up to $+0.054$). These comparisons are not independent: they share dense curves, trunks and
seeds. The margin shrinks with data per parameter: for the 10M middle block looped 4
times it is $+0.119$ at $40N$, $+0.062$ at $80N$ and $+0.016$ at $160N$. Trained at a lower learning rate (0.35 times the
dense rate, the lowest that S1 tried, where the loss still fell as the rate was lowered), the same model is level with the dense model
at $160N$ on average ($-0.011$ and $+0.005$ in two seeds, so in one seed the looped model is ahead by 0.011 nats): the
only measured equal-compute comparison in this study that the dense model does not win. The other 62 comparisons still need an extrapolated
dense loss: 43 at 40M--160M, and 19 at 10M--20M, almost all for the third seed, whose dense curve ends at $80N$. At 160M
and $40N$ the bracket reaches from $+0.004$ to $+0.043$. Within the measured
range, loops trade compute for parameters and data; they do not save compute.

\subsection{Learning rates}
\label{sec:lr}

All looped language models use the dense models' learning rates: a check of ten looped cells at 20M and $10N$, whose
decision rule was written before its runs, found no better one. It tried 0.5, 0.71 and 1 times the dense rate, and nine
of the ten cells were best at 1$\times$, the top of that range, so higher rates were not excluded. Because the check covered neither longer budgets
nor other sizes, we re-tuned both the dense model and the middle block looped 4 times at 10M, 20M and 40M
(supplementary experiment S1, \lbl{pre-specified}): 0.5 to 1.41 times the dense rate, plus 0.35 times where the best
rate lay at the lowest one tried, at $10N$--$40N$ (at 10M also $80N$ and $160N$), with one seed (two at 20M). The dense model's best rate stayed at 1$\times$, except at 40M, where 0.71$\times$ was
better by at most 0.004 nats. At 20M--40M the shared rate understated the looped
model's advantage by at most 0.005 nats; at 10M the looped model preferred 0.5$\times$ and below from $20N$ on, worth
about 0.02 nats. With each model at its best rate, the gap at $40N$ is $-0.005$, $-0.031$ and $-0.055$ nats from 10M to
40M (shared rate, same seeds: $+0.015$, $-0.031$, $-0.050$). The size trend is somewhat smaller but holds by the
criterion of the plan (the improvement from 10M to 40M at the best rates exceeds half of that at the shared rate); the
data trend at 10M is larger ($+0.082$ at $10N$ to $-0.059$ at $160N$). At equal compute the dense model won 10 of the 11
comparisons of the re-tuned runs (one of them, 40M at $40N$, on an extrapolated dense loss). The eleventh, at 10M and $160N$, first needed an extrapolated dense loss; measured in
S4 it is $-0.011$, a looped advantage of 0.011 nats, and with a second seed ($+0.005$) the two are level on average
(Section~\ref{sec:equalcompute}, Appendix~\ref{app:lr}).

\subsection{Where the gain lands, and what the seeds measure}
\label{sec:where}

Per token, the gain on web text is a small, broad shift: about half of the tokens improve, and the gain is not
concentrated on hard tokens beyond what two dense seeds differ by \lbl{(exploratory)}. On math and code text the gain is
larger, on average about 3 and 7 times the gain on the main set, but the seed-to-seed spread on those sets is 10--100
times larger as well. Supplementary experiment S7 \lbl{(pre-specified)} showed that the code set is even more sensitive to the data
order, which our seeds share, than to the initialisation (the math set is not): at 20M, training the same initialisation on three disjoint parts of the data stream moves
the main-set loss by only 0.003--0.011 nats, 0.1--0.5 times the spread between initialisation seeds (the plan had
predicted one to three times), but moves the code-set loss by 0.21--0.69 nats,
up to 7 times that spread, and changes the sign of the looped-minus-dense gap there. Of the plan's predictions, scored
as four, only this one, a code-set spread above 0.1, held: on the main set the data-order spread was smaller than
predicted relative to the initialisation spread (and, for the dense model, below the predicted 0.005--0.02 nats), and the
looped-minus-dense gap did not vary less than the losses. Both the initialisation and the documents a run happens to see
move its out-of-distribution loss strongly at this size, so we draw no conclusions from differences on those sets.
