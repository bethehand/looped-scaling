\section{What a loop is worth on text}
\label{sec:text}

\subsection{The pre-registered verdicts}
\label{sec:verdicts}

The dense ruler, fitted to the 54 dense results of the grid, puts its irreducible loss at the lower bound of the fit
($E = 0.050$) and its parameter exponent at $\alpha = 0.054$, far below the usual value of about 0.3; five sizes cannot
pin down $E$, $A$ and $\alpha$ separately. The joint fit over all 352 results gives $\alpha = 0.267$ and larger
$\varphi$. Table~\ref{tab:prereg} gives $\varphi$ per cell and the verdicts \lbl{(pre-registered)}.

Two observations from the grid matter for what follows. First, truncated backpropagation, predicted to hurt, helped
whole-stack loops: at 20M a whole stack looped 4 times reached 3.646 with truncation and 3.783 with full
backpropagation at $10N$ (three-seed means), and at 10M the only looped cells better than the dense model were
whole-stack loops with $r = 8$. Second, truncation and whole-stack loops brought instability: 30 of the 111 runs had
at least one loss spike, 25 of them with truncated backpropagation (16 of these whole-stack loops) and the other five
whole-stack loops with $r = 8$; one run collapsed. Halving the learning rate removed the spikes in a 10M follow-up
(Appendix~\ref{app:training}).

\subsection{Why $\varphi$ is not identified, and what to measure instead}
\label{sec:multiplier}

$\varphi$ converts a loss into effective parameters through the ruler, and the ruler is weakly identified: with the
primary fit $\varphi$ is 0--0.17 per cell, with the joint fit 0--0.41. Its trend across sizes is robust, though. With
the ruler fixed, the middle block looped 4 times has $\varphi = -0.32$, 0.19, 0.39, 0.52 and 0.65 from 10M to 160M
\lbl{(exploratory)}. A single $\varphi$ per cell averages over very different regimes.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig2_multiplier}
  \caption{What a parameter of a looped middle block is worth, measured without a fitted law: the multiplier
  $M = \Nstar/N$, where $\Nstar$ is the size of the dense model that reaches the same loss on the same tokens per
  parameter (interpolated between the measured dense sizes; open markers: beyond the largest or smallest dense size by
  at most a factor of two). $M = 1$: the looped model is as good as a dense model of its own size. Bars: 95\% intervals
  from resampling seeds (80M has one seed).}
  \label{fig:multiplier}
\end{figure}

The dense runs themselves can serve as the ruler. For each looped model we interpolate the loss of the measured
dense models trained on the same tokens per parameter and read off the dense size $\Nstar$ with the same loss
(Figure~\ref{fig:multiplier}, \lbl{exploratory}). The multiplier $M = \Nstar/N$ of the middle block looped 4 times rises
from 0.6--1.0 at 10--20M with 10--20 tokens per parameter to 1.26--1.46 at 40M--160M with 20--40; the 160M values lie
beyond the largest dense model, by less than a factor of two. Counting $N$ without the output head, as
\citet{schwethelm2026how} do, the local exponent there is $\varphi_{\mathrm{loc}} = 0.32$--0.50, close to their
$\varphi = 0.46$ (0.35--0.52 with the head). A least-squares
plane in $\log_2 M$ gives a factor of 1.21 per doubling of model size (95\% interval 1.13--1.30) and 1.14 per doubling of
tokens per parameter (1.05--1.24; 1.17 with 8 loops); the product term is indistinguishable from zero. The loop
breaks even ($M = 1$) at about 35M parameters with 20 tokens per parameter and about 11M with 80. Eight loops add
almost nothing over four at these sizes.

Published equal-parameter results restate to a similar range. Where a paper reports a non-looped ladder trained on the
same data, its looped models are worth 1.23--1.36 dense parameters per parameter for Ouro at 134M--374M
\citep[values read off plots]{zhu2025scaling}, 1.31--1.46 for Parcae at 140M--770M \citep{prairie2026parcae} and
1.26--1.36 for the 1B models of \citet{saunshi2025reasoning}; the law of \citet{schwethelm2026how} implies 1.25, 1.45 and
1.56 for 2, 4 and 8 loops. These studies differ from ours in size, data and recipe, so the agreement is a consistency
check, not a pooled estimate. They barely sample models below 40M trained on 20 or fewer tokens per parameter, where we
find a loop worth at most about its parameters. That the value rises with tokens per parameter at equal parameters is
an interaction that effective-parameter laws with one exponent per architecture
\citep{schwethelm2026how,chen2026moe} do not capture; it agrees with the recommendation of \citet{prairie2026parcae} to
increase looping and data in tandem, and with the finding of \citet{chen2026growth} that with repeated data the best
loop count grows with compute.

\subsection{At equal training compute, the dense model wins by less and less}
\label{sec:equalcompute}

At equal parameters, a middle block looped 2, 4 or 8 times costs 1.4--1.5, 2.2--2.4 or 3.7--4.1 times the training FLOPs
per token of the dense model. For an equal-compute comparison we give the dense model the tokens it could have seen
with the looped run's compute and read its loss off the measured dense curve. Of the 320 looped results of the grid and
its extensions, 221 could be compared this way with the original dense runs; the dense model won all of them, by at
least 0.016 nats. The other 99 needed the dense loss beyond its measured curve. Two extrapolations bracket it: in a
backtest that drops the last budgets of every measured dense curve, extending the last segment predicts a dense loss
0.023--0.036 nats too low one doubling out (it flatters the dense model), and a power law fitted to the whole curve one
0.006--0.027 too high (it flatters looping). With the power law, looping came out ahead in 6 of the 99, by at most
0.021 nats, all at the largest data per parameter.

We then continued the 10M dense models to $640N$ and the 20M ones to $320N$ (supplementary experiment S4,
\lbl{pre-specified}), which turns 37 of the extrapolated comparisons into measured ones (Figure~\ref{fig:equalcompute},
Table~\ref{tab:equalcompute}). The dense model wins all 258 measured comparisons, and the measured gaps fall about
halfway between the two extrapolations. These comparisons are not independent: they share dense curves, trunks and
seeds. The margin shrinks with data per parameter: for the 10M middle block looped 4
times it is $+0.119$ at $40N$, $+0.062$ at $80N$ and $+0.016$ at $160N$. Trained at its own learning rate (0.35 times the
dense rate, found in S1), the same model reaches parity at $160N$ ($-0.011$ and $+0.005$ in two seeds), the only
equal-compute comparison in this study that is not a dense win. The other 62 comparisons still need an extrapolated
dense loss: 43 at 40M--160M, and 19 at 10M--20M, almost all for the third seed, whose dense curve ends at $80N$. At 160M
and $40N$ the bracket reaches from $+0.004$ to $+0.043$. Within the measured
range, loops trade compute for parameters and data; they do not save compute.

\subsection{Learning rates}
\label{sec:lr}

All looped language models use the dense models' learning rates, after a check of ten looped cells at 20M and $10N$,
with its decision rule written before its runs, found no better one. Because the check covered neither longer budgets
nor other sizes, we re-tuned both the dense model and the middle block looped 4 times at 10M, 20M and 40M
(supplementary experiment S1, \lbl{pre-specified}): 0.5 to 1.41 times the dense rate, plus 0.35 times where the best
rate lay at the lowest one tried, at every budget used above, with one seed (two at 20M). The dense model's best rate stayed at 1$\times$. At 20M--40M the shared rate understated the looped
model's advantage by at most 0.005 nats; at 10M the looped model preferred 0.5$\times$ and below from $20N$ on, worth
about 0.02 nats. With each model at its best rate, the gap at $40N$ is $-0.005$, $-0.031$ and $-0.055$ nats from 10M to
40M (shared rate, same seeds: $+0.015$, $-0.031$, $-0.050$). The size trend is somewhat smaller but holds by the
criterion of the plan (the improvement from 10M to 40M at the best rates exceeds half of that at the shared rate); the
data trend at 10M is larger ($+0.082$ at $10N$ to $-0.059$ at $160N$). At equal compute the dense model won all 11
comparisons of the re-tuned runs, but the one at 10M and $160N$ needed an extrapolated dense loss; measured in S4, it is
the parity reported above (Appendix~\ref{app:lr}).

\subsection{Where the gain lands, and what the seeds measure}
\label{sec:where}

Per token, the gain on web text is a small, broad shift: about half of the tokens improve, and the gain is not
concentrated on hard tokens beyond what two dense seeds differ by \lbl{(exploratory)}. On math and code text the gain is
larger, on average about 3 and 7 times the gain on the main set, but the seed-to-seed spread on those sets is 10--100
times larger as well. Supplementary experiment S7 \lbl{(pre-specified)} traced that spread to the data order: at 20M,
training the same initialisation on three disjoint parts of the data stream moves the main-set loss by only
0.003--0.011 nats, 0.1--0.5 times the spread between initialisation seeds, but moves the code-set loss by 0.21--0.69 nats,
up to 7 times that spread, and changes the sign of the looped-minus-dense gap there. Which documents a run happens to
see decides its out-of-distribution loss at this size, so we draw no conclusions from differences on those sets.
