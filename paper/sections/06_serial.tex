\section{Loops as extendable depth on a serial task}
\label{sec:serial}

Predicting the next word of web text rarely needs many serial steps, so text gives the loop little reason to iterate.
We therefore built a task that does, froze its design, its conditions and its predictions before the main runs, and
ran it with the same architecture. Pilot runs then led to three changes before any formal run, all for learnability:
the operations became lookup tables instead of modular arithmetic, a curriculum was added, and training was
lengthened; the learning-rate protocol was fixed after the pilot (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).

\subsection{The task and the conditions}
\label{sec:task}

A problem lists a start value and a sequence of table lookups, for example \texttt{25 ; LUT 7 ; LUT 3 ; LUT 19 ; \ldots\
=>}, where each \texttt{LUT k} is one of 32 fixed random functions on $\{0,\ldots,96\}$. The model must output the final
value in a single forward pass; the loss is taken at the answer position only. Two lookups compose into one of 1024
arbitrary functions with no compact form, so a model that skips steps must memorise rapidly growing tables of
compositions; the task rewards computing the chain step by step, although, as for any associative composition, a
shallower solution exists in principle \citep{liu2022shortcuts}. A prompt with $d$ steps has $3d + 4$ tokens. Models are trained on 1--8 steps, sampled online with a curriculum that
widens the range over the first half of training, for 32{,}000 steps of 512 problems, and are tested on 2000 fresh
problems at every length from 1 to 24. Chains longer than 8 steps never occur in training; short problems do repeat,
since there are only 3{,}104 one-step problems.

All models have width 256. The conditions are dense models of 8, 20 and 36 layers (6.5M, 16.1M and 29.0M parameters)
and 8-layer looped models (6.6M parameters, a middle block of four layers looped $r$ times) trained in four regimes:
with $r = 2$, 4 or 8 loops; with a random loop count drawn from 1--8 at every step; and with as many loops as the chain
has steps (``loops = steps'', the regime of \citet{fan2025looped}). The 20- and 36-layer dense models match the executed
depth of the looped models with 4 and 8 loops, with 2.4 and 4.4 times their parameters. Each condition's learning rate
was chosen by a sweep on in-range accuracy only, so that the choice cannot favour extrapolation; the looped rates
(1.25--2.5$\times10^{-4}$) came out 2--8 times lower than the dense ones (5$\times10^{-4}$--$10^{-3}$). Every condition has
five seeds. The design fixed five predictions (P1--P5, Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}); a planned pointer-chasing control
was dropped because no condition learned it under the chain-calibrated recipe.

More loops at inference solve longer chains (Figure~\ref{fig:chain}b). The model trained with 8 loops solves 9 steps
at 8 loops and about 11 with 16 or 32; at 12 steps its accuracy rises from 0.04 to 0.86 with the best larger loop count
(0.83 at 16 loops, on average). The gain is real but bounded: at 16 steps the best is 0.37, with a wide spread across
seeds. The model trained with a random loop count gains as well (0.08 to 0.49 at 16 steps), against the frozen
prediction that it would not. The model trained with loops = steps, run with as many loops as the chain has steps,
solves the longest chains at accuracy 0.9 (12.8 steps) and beats 8 loops by 0.34--0.95 at every length from 10 to 16, as
predicted, partly because the problem states its number of steps; at 16 steps the random-loop model with 16 loops is
more accurate (0.49 against 0.37).

\subsection{One step per loop}
\label{sec:probes}

For each intermediate value $x_j$ we train a linear probe on the state after every loop, at the input positions of
step $j$, on problems with 8 steps (Figure~\ref{fig:probes}). The \emph{frontier} is the first loop at which each
value becomes decodable (probe accuracy $\geq 0.5$), and \emph{persistence} is the share of decoded values that stay
decodable in the loops that follow. In the model trained with 8 loops, $x_j$ first becomes decodable at about loop $j$
(1.0--1.1 loops per step in all five seeds) and stays decodable afterwards (persistence 0.96 within its loops). The loops = steps model also computes one step per loop and keeps its values for its $d$ loops
(0.92), but loses them when run longer: it was never trained to keep looping after the last step, which is why it needs
exactly $d$ loops. Models with fewer loops than steps pack more steps into each loop, about 1.6 per loop with 4 loops
and 2.4 with 2, the direction predicted by the budget law of \citet{zhang2026recurrence}. The dense models compute late (from about layer 21 of 36),
and in 5 of the 10 seeds of the 20- and 36-layer models no intermediate value appears at the step positions at all,
only the answer at the last layer. Measured by the share of values that first become decodable at their own loop, the
loops = steps and 8-loop models are diagonal (0.95 and 0.97) and the others are not (0.12).

\subsection{The learning rate decides whether the loop learns the task}
\label{sec:lrsynthetic}

In the learning-rate sweep, the 8-loop model trained at $10^{-3}$, a good rate for the dense models, worked only at
exactly 8 loops and had no step-by-step frontier, like the language models of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}; but it had
also learned the training range much worse (0.54 against 0.999 at $2.5\times10^{-4}$). Probes of all sweep checkpoints
show the frontier at rates up to $5\times10^{-4}$ and not at $10^{-3}$ or $2\times10^{-3}$, for 4 and 8 loops and the
random loop count alike. We then trained the four extrapolating looped conditions (supplementary experiment S2,
\lbl{pre-specified}) at $5\times10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$ for the full 32{,}000 steps with three seeds. At $10^{-3}$, 10 of 12 runs did
not learn the training range in that time (accuracy 0.49--0.98); at $5\times10^{-4}$, 8 of 12 did, and those mostly
extended about as far as the formal runs (8 loops: 2.7 steps beyond the training range against 3.2; loops = steps: 4.0
against 4.8), except the random loop count, whose two learned seeds extended 2 steps against 4.2. A high learning rate
mainly keeps the looped model from learning the task in the given steps; when it learns the task, it mostly learns the
same extendable algorithm.

\subsection{Knowing when to stop}
\label{sec:stopping}

Extra loops help only if the model can tell how many to run. The frozen design specified one answer-free rule: stop
when the state at the answer position changes by less than 2\% between loops. We compared it (supplementary experiment
S8, \lbl{pre-specified}), on the same 25 checkpoints, with rules that stop when the prediction has not changed for $m$
loops or when the model's top probability reaches a threshold $\tau$ (the form of the maximum-confidence rule of
\citealp{fan2025looped} that can stop early), and with majority votes over loop counts; the rules and thresholds were fixed in the plan, selected on one problem set and
reported on another. Table~\ref{tab:stopping} and Figure~\ref{fig:stopping} give the results.

The state rule recovers about 80\% of the gain of the best fixed loop count at 12 steps for the models trained with 4
or 8 loops, without hurting the training range, but it cannot stop the loops = steps model, whose state keeps changing
after $d$ loops: it stops on a wrong loop at every length (0.03--0.08). The model's own confidence does better, and it
is the best of the rules that stop in every condition. (The plan's selection rule, the highest accuracy over 9--16 steps
on the selection set among rules that keep the in-range accuracy within 0.01 of the training loop count's, also
admitted votes over loop counts and picked a vote for three of the five conditions; a vote always runs all 32 loops and
only ties the best fixed count.) At a threshold of 0.99 the confidence rule recovers 97--98\% of the best fixed count's gain at 12 steps for the models trained with 4 or 8
loops or a random count, using fewer loops than their training count on in-range problems, and it brings the loops =
steps model to 0.76 at a cost of 6--7\% of the in-range problems. Stopping when the prediction stops changing fires on
stable wrong answers, because the fixed-loop models turn their read-out only in the last loop; votes over loop counts tie
the best fixed count but never beat it, and they are not stopping rules.

The models that extend furthest and the models that stop well thus came from different training regimes. We therefore
trained the loops = steps model with 0--4 random extra loops after the last step (supplementary experiment S9,
\lbl{pre-specified}). It
keeps its values when run longer (persistence 0.04 $\rightarrow$ 0.91), extends about one step less (11.6 against 12.8),
and stops by confidence at 0.93 on 12-step problems while keeping 0.998 of the training range, using fewer loops than $d$
in range. By the frozen state rule its stop is seed-dependent (0.52: it works in two seeds and fails in two), an
outcome that none of the three interpretations written into the plan in advance covered. Training it costs about 1.4 times the compute per step.

\subsection{Against reasoning written out}
\label{sec:cot}

We gave the same problems to dense models that write the intermediate values out as tokens before the answer
\citep{wei2022chain}, with 8 layers (the looped models' size) and 36 layers (their executed depth), and to an 8-layer
model with $d$ pause tokens before the answer \citep{goyal2024think} (supplementary experiment S5,
\lbl{pre-specified}); five seeds each, learning rates chosen as for the other conditions. Both chain-of-thought models
scored 1.000 in range at every rate tried, so the protocol's tie-break gave them the lowest one.

Within the training range the chain of thought is perfect, 1.000 in every seed and at every learning rate tried, where
the 8-layer model that answers directly solves 4 steps; and it costs a third of the looped model's inference compute
at its training loop count (Table~\ref{tab:cot}). Beyond the range it does not extend at all: 0.014 at 12 steps with 8
layers and 0.017 with 36, chance level, against 0.86--0.96 for the looped models on the same prompts
(Figure~\ref{fig:chain}c). A diagnostic \lbl{(exploratory)} shows where it breaks: as soon as the prompt holds more than 8
tables, the first wrong token is one of the first three written ones in 97--98\% of the problems for the 8-layer model,
and this persists when the correct earlier tokens are given. The failure is one of generalising to longer inputs, a
known weakness of transformers \citep{zhou2024transformers,cho2024position}, not an accumulation of errors along the
chain. Because the prompt of this task grows with the number of steps, the comparison cannot say whether a written
chain would extend on a task whose input length does not grow, and remedies for length generalisation were outside the
plan. Pause tokens add nothing: the pause model solves the same 4 steps as the model without them.

So on this task the question ``loops or tokens?'' has two answers. For problems like the training ones, tokens: cheaper
and perfect. For longer ones, loops: the only form of extra computation here that extended.

\subsection{Against the pre-specified predictions}
\label{sec:predictions}

The frozen design made five predictions. P1 (more loops, more steps at equal parameters) holds in direction, but the
8-loop model did not reach the predicted 16 steps. P2 (dense models of equal depth at least as good) is not supported as
stated, with small gaps of 1--1.6 steps in favour of the loop. P3 (inference-time behaviour) fails in its first two parts,
in the direction that favours looping (fixed-loop models were not hurt by extra loops, and the random-loop model, predicted
not to gain, gained), and holds in its third (the loops = steps model extends). P4 (only the loops = steps model computes
along the diagonal) is half right, since the 8-loop model does as well. P5 (much larger gaps than on text) holds. Of the
supplementary predictions, S5 held one of three, S8 one of five and S9 three of four (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}).
