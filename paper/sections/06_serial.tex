\section{On a serial task, a loop buys extendable depth}
\label{sec:serial}

We built a task that needs serial steps (the chain task), froze its design, its conditions and its predictions before the main runs, and
ran it with the same architecture. Pilot runs then led to three changes before any formal run, all for learnability:
the operations became lookup tables instead of modular arithmetic, a curriculum was added, and training was
lengthened; the learning-rate protocol was fixed after the pilot (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}). We first ask what the
loop buys at its training loop count (Section~\ref{sec:inrange}), then what extra loops buy (Section~\ref{sec:extension}).

\subsection{The task and the conditions}
\label{sec:task}

A problem lists a start value and a sequence of table lookups (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}b), for example, after a
start marker, \texttt{25 ; LUT 7 ; LUT 3 ; LUT 19 ; \ldots\ =>}, where each \texttt{LUT k} is one of 32 fixed random functions on $\{0,\ldots,96\}$. The model must output the final
value in a single forward pass; the loss is taken at the answer position only. Two lookups compose into one of 1024
arbitrary functions, so a model that skips steps must memorise rapidly growing tables of compositions, although, as for
any associative composition, a shallower solution exists in principle \citep{liu2022shortcuts}. A prompt with $d$ steps has $3d + 4$ tokens. Models
are trained on 1--8 steps, sampled online with a curriculum that widens the range over the first half of training, for
32{,}000 steps of 512 problems, and are tested on 2{,}000 fresh problems at every length from 1 to 24. Chains longer than 8
steps never occur in training; short problems do repeat, since there are only 3{,}104 one-step problems.

All models have width 256. The conditions are dense models of 8, 20 and 36 layers (6.5M, 16.1M and 29.0M parameters)
and 8-layer looped models (6.6M parameters, a middle block of four layers looped $r$ times) trained with $r = 2$, 4 or 8
loops, with a random loop count drawn from 1--8 at every step, or with as many loops as the chain has steps (``loops =
steps'', the regime of \citet{fan2025looped}); a fourth regime, loops = steps with a few random extra loops (S9), follows
in Section~\ref{sec:stopping}. The 20- and 36-layer dense models match the executed depth of the looped models with 4 and
8 loops. Supplementary experiment S5 \lbl{(pre-specified)} added dense models that write the values of the chain out as
tokens, the last of them the answer, a chain of thought \citep{wei2022chain} trained from scratch on the written values as in
scratchpad training \citep{nye2021show}, with 8 layers (the looped models' size) and 36 (the 8-loop model's executed depth), and an
8-layer model with $d$ pause tokens before the answer \citep{goyal2024think}. Each condition's learning rate was chosen by
a sweep on in-range accuracy only; the looped rates came out 4--8 times lower than that of the 8-layer dense model,
which has the same unique layers (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}). Every condition has five seeds. The design fixed five
predictions (P1--P5, Section~\ref{sec:predictions}); a planned pointer-chasing control was not learned by any condition
tried on it and was dropped (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}).

\subsection{Within the training range, a loop buys depth, and a written chain of thought buys it more cheaply}
\label{sec:inrange}

Two caveats frame this comparison. A dense model of equal executed depth has the same width and is trained for the same
steps on the same problems, so on this task equal executed depth means about the same training and inference compute, at
2.4--4.4 times the parameters. And the chain-of-thought models are trained with a loss on every intermediate value, the other
models on the answer only, a difference the plan stated in advance; a looped model with intermediate supervision was
outside the plan. The first caveat favours the loop (its comparator has more parameters), the second the chain of
thought (more supervision).

At equal parameters, more loops solve longer chains: 4.2, 6.4, 8.0 and 9.0 steps for the dense model and 2, 4 and 8
loops (Table~\ref{tab:chain}, \lbl{pre-specified}); within the training range the 8-loop model is at 0.998 where the
8-layer dense model is at 0.62. At equal executed depth, and so at equal compute, the loop is at least as good: the
20-layer dense model solves 6.4 steps against 8.0 for 4 loops, though it did not fully learn the training range (0.91),
and the 36-layer one 8.0 against 9.0 for 8 loops, level within the range (the lengths at the training loop count are
the plan's quantity; the ``more'' columns are read in Section~\ref{sec:extension}). Unlike on text, where the dense model of equal compute
has the looped model's size and more data, here it has 2.4--4.4 times the parameters and the same data, and it does not
beat the loop; we do not read these two comparisons as a saving of compute, since one dense model did not fully learn
the range and the other is level within it. A written chain of thought is cheaper: it is perfect
within the training range, 1.000 in every seed and at every learning rate tried, where the 8-layer model that answers
directly solves 4 steps, and it needs under a third of the inference compute of the 8-loop model at any length (416
against 1440 layer passes at 12 steps). Pause tokens add nothing: the pause model solves the same 4 steps as the model
without them. Within the training range, the loop buys serial depth at the price of its executed layers, and written
tokens buy it more cheaply.

\subsection{Beyond the training range, only the loop extends}
\label{sec:extension}

All answer-only dense models fail right after the training range: at 12 steps they score 0.02--0.06, and so do the
fixed-loop models at their training loop count, 0.03--0.05 (Figure~\ref{fig:chain}a). Run with more loops, the looped models solve longer
chains (Figure~\ref{fig:chain}b). The model trained with 8 loops solves 9 steps at 8 loops and about 11 with 16 or 32; at
12 steps its accuracy rises from 0.04 to 0.86 with the best loop count of 12--32 chosen per seed (0.83 at 16 loops, on
average). The gain is real but bounded: at 16 steps the best is 0.37, with a wide spread across seeds. The model trained
with a random loop count gains as well (0.08 to 0.49 at 16 steps), against the frozen prediction that it would not. The
model trained with loops = steps, run with as many loops as the chain has steps, solves the longest chains at accuracy
0.9 (12.8 steps) and beats 8 loops by 0.34--0.95 at every length from 10 to 16 (counting from 10 steps, a reading fixed
after the results; at 9 steps the gain is 0.03), partly because the problem states its number of steps.

The written chain of thought does not extend at all: 0.014 at 12 steps with 8 layers and 0.017 with 36, near the chance
level of 0.010, on prompts on which the looped models trained with 4 or more loops reach 0.83--0.96 (Figure~\ref{fig:chain}c). It breaks at the
start: as soon as the prompt holds more than 8 tables, the first wrong token is one of the first three written ones in
97--98\% of the problems for the 8-layer model \lbl{(exploratory)}. The failure is one of generalising to longer inputs,
a known weakness of transformers \citep{zhou2024transformers,cho2024position}, not an accumulation of errors along the
chain. Because the prompt of this task grows with the number of steps, the comparison cannot say whether a written chain
would extend on a task whose input length does not grow, and remedies for length generalisation were outside the plan.
Pause tokens do not extend either.

Together with Section~\ref{sec:inrange}, this is what the loop buys on this task: not the cheapest computation within
the training range, but depth that extends beyond it. What the answer-only dense models lack is not depth but the option to
run longer; the chain of thought has that option and fails on the longer prompt.

\subsection{The loop computes the chain step by step}
\label{sec:probes}

For each intermediate value $x_j$ we trained a linear probe on the state after every loop, at the input positions of
step $j$, on problems with 8 steps (planned in the frozen design; the summaries are \lbl{exploratory};
Figure~\ref{fig:probes}). In the model trained with 8 loops, $x_j$ first becomes decodable (probe accuracy $\geq 0.5$) at
about loop $j$, 1.0--1.1 loops per step in all five seeds, and stays decodable afterwards (lowest later accuracy,
averaged over the values, 0.96). The loops = steps model also computes one step per loop and keeps its values for its $d$
loops (0.92), but loses them when run longer: it was never trained to keep looping after the last step, which is why it
needs exactly $d$ loops. Models with fewer loops than steps pack about 1.6 steps into each loop with 4 loops and 2.4 with
2, the direction predicted by the budget law of \citet{zhang2026recurrence}. The dense models compute late (from about
layer 21 of 36), and in 5 of their 10 seeds at 20 and 36 layers the probes read no intermediate value after the first. This
would explain the extension: a loop that applies the same step each time can be run for more steps, a stack that
computes the chain in its last layers cannot. Probes show where a value can be read out, not that the model uses it.

\subsection{The learning rate decides whether the loop learns the task within the budget}
\label{sec:lrsynthetic}

At $10^{-3}$, a good rate for the dense models, the 8-loop model of the learning-rate sweep worked at no other loop count from 2
to 32 and had no step-by-step frontier (no intermediate value became decodable one loop per step), like the language models of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, but it had also learned the training range much worse (0.54 against 0.999 at
$2.5\times10^{-4}$). Trained for 32{,}000 steps with three seeds (supplementary experiment S2, \lbl{pre-specified}), the
four looped conditions that its plan had identified as extrapolating did not learn the training range in 10 of 12 runs
at $10^{-3}$ and learned it in 8 of 12 at $5\times10^{-4}$, and those mostly extended about as far as the formal runs
(Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}). A high learning rate mainly keeps the looped model from learning the task in the given
steps; when it learns the task, it mostly learns the same extendable algorithm (by the plan's own readings the learning
rate changed the solution in one of the eight cells, the random loop count at $5\times10^{-4}$;
Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}).

\subsection{In most training regimes, the model's own confidence tells it when to stop}
\label{sec:stopping}

Extra loops help only if the model can tell how many to run. The frozen design specified one answer-free rule, for the
loops = steps model: stop when the state changes by less than a threshold between loops, implemented at the freeze as a
relative change below 2\% at the answer position, with at most 32 loops. Supplementary experiment S8
(\lbl{pre-specified}; planned after this rule's results were known, frozen before the comparison) compared it on the
same 25 checkpoints with rules that stop when the prediction has not changed for $m$ loops or when the model's top
probability reaches a threshold (an early-stopping variant of the maximum-confidence rule of \citealp{fan2025looped}, which they mention but do not evaluate),
and with votes over loop counts, all fixed in the plan, selected on one problem set and reported on another
(Table~\ref{tab:stopping}).

The state rule recovers about 80\% of the gain of the best fixed loop count at 12 steps for the models trained with 4 or
8 loops, without hurting the training range, but it cannot stop the loops = steps model, whose state keeps changing after
$d$ loops (0.02--0.08 at every length). The model's own confidence does better: at a threshold of 0.99 it recovers 97--98\% of that gain for the
models trained with 4 or 8 loops or a random count, using fewer loops than their training count on in-range problems,
and brings the loops = steps model to 0.76 at a cost of 6--7\% of the in-range problems. Among the rules that stop, a confidence rule (at 0.99, or at 0.9 for $r = 2$) is the best in every condition of S8
except $r = 4$, where a state rule with a lower threshold (0.5\%) ties the confidence rule (0.816 against 0.815) with
far more loops (27 against 9). Stopping when the prediction stops changing fires on stable wrong
answers, and votes over loop counts run all 32 loops and never beat the best fixed count; the plan's selection criterion
picked a vote for three of the five conditions and, without votes, the confidence rule at 0.99 only for $r = 8$ and the
random loop count (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}).

The models that extend furthest and the models that stop well thus came from different training regimes. Trained with
0--4 random extra loops after the last step (supplementary experiment S9, \lbl{pre-specified}), the loops = steps model
keeps its values when run longer (lowest probe accuracy over 16 loops 0.04 $\rightarrow$ 0.91), extends about one step
less (11.6 against 12.8), and stops by confidence at 0.93 on 12-step problems (0.89--0.95 in the five seeds) while
keeping 0.998 of the training range;
the 0.5\% state rule is slightly better (0.94, in all five seeds) with 24 loops against 14. By the frozen state rule its
stop is seed-dependent (0.52: it works in two seeds, fails in two and half-works in the fifth), an outcome none of the
three interpretations written into the plan covered. Training it costs about 1.4 times the compute per step.

\subsection{Against the pre-specified predictions}
\label{sec:predictions}

Of the five predictions of the frozen design (Table~\ref{tab:p1p5} in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}), P5 (much larger
gaps than on text) holds; P1 (more loops, more steps) holds in direction, but the 8-loop model did not reach the predicted
16 steps; P2 (dense models of equal depth at least as good) is not supported as stated, with gaps of 1--1.6 steps in
favour of the loop; P3 (inference-time behaviour) is half right in its first part, fails in its second in the direction
that favours looping (the random-loop model gained) and holds in its third (the loops = steps model extends); and P4
(only the loops = steps model computes along the diagonal) is half right, since the 8-loop model does as well, though by
the design's literal reading P4 would hold. The readings of P3's third part and of P4 were fixed after the results. Of
the supplementary predictions on this task, S5 held one of three, S8 two of five and S9 three of four.

\paragraph{What the loop buys on a serial task.} On a task that needs serial steps, the same architecture computes the
chain step by step inside the loop (by linear probes), one step per loop when it has a loop for every step. Within the training range this buys depth, at no less compute than a dense model of the same
executed depth and at more than a written chain of thought; beyond it, the loop is the only form of extra computation
here that extends. It learns to do so when its learning rate is low enough for the task to be learned in the budget, and,
for most training regimes, its own confidence tells it when to stop.
