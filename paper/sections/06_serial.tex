\section{On a serial task, a loop buys extendable depth}
\label{sec:serial}

We built a task that needs serial steps (the chain task), froze its design, conditions and predictions before the main
runs, and ran it with the same architecture. Pilot runs then led to three changes before any formal run, all to make the
task learnable: the operations became lookup tables instead of modular arithmetic, a curriculum was added, and training
was lengthened. Training was lengthened after a one-seed pilot of all conditions, and the learning-rate protocol was fixed after that
(Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}). We first ask what the loop gives at its training loop count
(Section~\ref{sec:inrange}), then what extra loops give (Section~\ref{sec:extension}).

\subsection{The task and the conditions}
\label{sec:task}

\paragraph{The task.} A problem lists a start value and a sequence of table lookups (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}b). For example, after a start marker, \texttt{25 ; LUT 7 ; LUT 3 ; LUT 19 ; \ldots\ =>}. Here \texttt{LUT k} means ``look up the
current value in table $T_k$'', one of 32 fixed random tables that map $\{0,\ldots,96\}$ to itself. Each lookup is one
step: the $j$-th gives the intermediate value $x_j$, and a chain of $d$ steps has $d$ lookups. The model must output the
final value in a single forward pass, and the loss is taken at the answer position only. Two consecutive lookups can form
any of the $32 \times 32 = 1024$ compositions of two tables, so a model that skips steps must memorise tables of
compositions, which grow rapidly with the number of steps skipped. Because composition is associative, the chain could
in principle be computed in fewer serial steps than it has lookups \citep{liu2022shortcuts}. A prompt with $d$ steps has $3d + 4$ tokens.

\paragraph{Training and testing.} Models are trained on chains of 1--8 steps, sampled online with a curriculum that
widens the range over the first half of training, for 32{,}000 training steps with batches of 512 problems. They are tested on 2{,}000 newly
drawn problems at every length from 1 to 24. Chains longer than 8 steps never occur in training, but short ones repeat:
every test problem with 1 or 2 steps, and about half of those with 3, also occurs in training (there are only 3{,}104
one-step problems).

\paragraph{The conditions.} All models have width 256.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Dense models} of 8, 20 and 36 layers (6.5M, 16.1M and 29.0M parameters). The 20- and 36-layer models
  match the executed depth of the looped models with 4 and 8 loops.
  \item \emph{Looped models} with 8 layers (6.6M parameters, a middle block of four layers looped $r$ times), trained
  with $r = 2$, 4 or 8 loops; with a random loop count drawn from 1--8 at every training step; or with as many loops as the chain
  has steps (``loops = steps'', the regime of \citealp{fan2025looped}). A fourth regime, loops = steps with a few random
  extra loops (S9), follows in Section~\ref{sec:stopping}.
  \item \emph{Written reasoning} (S5, \lbl{pre-specified}): dense models that write the values of the chain out as
  tokens, the last of them the answer. This chain of thought \citep{wei2022chain} is trained from scratch on the written
  values, as in scratchpad training \citep{nye2021show}, with 8 layers (the looped models' size) and with 36 (the 8-loop
  model's executed depth). We also train an 8-layer dense model given $d$ pause tokens before the answer \citep{goyal2024think}.
\end{itemize}
Each condition's learning rate was chosen by a sweep on in-range accuracy only. The looped rates came out 4--8 times lower
than that of the 8-layer dense model, which has the same unique layers (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}). Every condition
has five seeds. The design fixed five predictions (P1--P5, Section~\ref{sec:predictions}). A planned control task, pointer chasing, was not learned by any condition tried on it and was dropped (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}).

\subsection{Within the training range, a loop adds depth, and a written chain of thought does so more cheaply}
\label{sec:inrange}

Two features of the setup matter for these comparisons. First, a dense model of equal executed depth has the same width
and is trained for the same number of training steps on the same problems. On this task, equal executed depth therefore
means about the same training and inference compute, but the dense model has 2.4--4.4 times the parameters. If the
extra parameters matter, they help the dense model, not the loop. Second, the chain-of-thought models are trained with a
loss on every intermediate value, the other models on the answer only, a difference the plan stated in advance. This
helps the chain of thought. A looped model with intermediate supervision was outside the plan.

\paragraph{At equal parameters, more loops solve longer chains.} The dense model and the models with 2, 4 and 8 loops
have largest solvable lengths of 4.2, 6.4, 8.0 and 9.0 steps (seed means; Table~\ref{tab:chain}, \lbl{pre-specified}). Within the training range, the 8-loop
model is at 0.998 where the 8-layer dense model is at 0.62.

\paragraph{At equal executed depth, and so at equal compute, the loop is at least as good.} The 20-layer dense model
solves 6.4 steps against 8.0 for 4 loops, though it did not fully learn the training range (0.91). Its learning rate was chosen in a sweep whose runs were half as long as the formal runs, and in that
sweep it reached 0.98 (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}). The 36-layer dense model
solves 8.0 steps against 9.0 for 8 loops, and the two are level within the range. The plan's measure is the solvable length at the training loop count;
Section~\ref{sec:extension} discusses the \emph{More loops} columns. Unlike on text, where the
dense model of equal compute has the looped model's size and more data, here it has 2.4--4.4 times the parameters and
the same data, and it does not beat the loop. We do not take these two comparisons as evidence that the loop saves compute, since one
dense model did not fully learn the range and the other is level within it.

\paragraph{A written chain of thought is cheaper.} It is perfect within the training range: 1.000 in every seed and at
every learning rate tried, where the 8-layer model that answers directly solves 4 steps. It needs under a third of the
inference compute of the 8-loop model at any length: 408 against 1440 layer passes at 12 steps
(Table~\ref{tab:chain}). Pause tokens add
nothing: the pause model solves the same 4 steps as the model without them.

Within the training range, then, the loop adds serial depth at the compute cost of its executed layers, and a written
chain of thought adds it at lower cost.

\subsection{Beyond the training range, only the loop extends}
\label{sec:extension}

We say that a model \emph{extends} if, given more computation at inference, it solves chains longer than those it was
trained on.

\paragraph{At their training loop count, dense and fixed-loop models stop at the range.} None of them gets more than one
step past the training range. At 12 steps, the dense models that answer directly score 0.01--0.06, and the fixed-loop
models 0.03--0.05 (Figure~\ref{fig:chain}a).

\paragraph{Run with more loops, looped models solve longer chains} (Figure~\ref{fig:chain}b). The model trained with 8
loops solves 9 steps at 8 loops and about 11 with 16 or 32. At 12 steps its accuracy rises from 0.04 to 0.83
at 16 loops, and to 0.86 when the best loop count between 12 and 32 is chosen for each seed using the answers (seed
means). The gain is limited: at 16 steps the
best is 0.37, with a wide spread across seeds. The model trained with a random loop count gains as well (0.08 to 0.49 at
16 steps), against the frozen prediction that it would not. The model trained with loops = steps, run with as many loops
as the chain has steps, solves the longest chains at accuracy 0.9 (12.8 steps). Part of this comes from being told how long
to run: its loop count is the number of steps, which the prompt shows. Run with $d$ loops, it is 0.34--0.95 more accurate
than run with 8 at every length from 10 to 16 (we count from 10 steps, a choice made after the results; at 9 steps the
difference is 0.03).

\paragraph{The written chain of thought does not extend at all.} It scores 0.014 at 12 steps with 8 layers and 0.017
with 36, near the chance level of 0.010, on prompts on which the looped models trained with 4 or more loops reach
0.83--0.96 (Figure~\ref{fig:chain}c). It breaks at the start: on problems with more than 8 steps, the first wrong token is one of the first three written ones in 97--98\% of the problems for the 8-layer model
\lbl{(exploratory)}. So the failure is one of generalising to longer inputs, a known weakness of transformers
\citep{zhou2024transformers,cho2024position}, not an accumulation of errors along the chain. Because the prompt of this
task grows with the number of steps, the comparison cannot say whether a written chain would extend on a task whose input
length does not grow, and remedies for length generalisation were outside the plan. Pause tokens do not extend either.

Together with Section~\ref{sec:inrange}, this is what the loop buys on this task: depth that extends beyond the
training range, though not the cheapest computation within it. The dense models that answer directly cannot run longer,
even with the 8-loop model's executed depth; the chain of thought can run longer but fails on the longer prompt.

\subsection{The loop computes the chain step by step}
\label{sec:probes}

For each intermediate value $x_j$, we trained a linear probe on the state after every loop, on problems with 8 steps
(planned in the frozen design; the summaries are \lbl{exploratory}; Figure~\ref{fig:probes}). Each probe reads three
prompt positions around step $j$, its table number, the separator after it and the next lookup token, and the best of
the three counts.

\paragraph{One step per loop.} In the model trained with 8 loops, $x_j$ first becomes decodable (probe accuracy
$\geq 0.5$) at about loop $j$, 1.0--1.1 loops per step in all five seeds. It stays decodable afterwards: its lowest probe accuracy at later
loops is 0.96, averaged over the values. The
loops = steps model also computes one step per loop and keeps its values for its $d$ loops (lowest later accuracy
0.92). But it loses them when run longer: it was never trained to keep looping after the last step,
which is why it needs exactly $d$ loops.

\paragraph{Several steps per loop, with fewer loops.} Probed, like all looped models, while running 16 loops, models with
fewer loops than steps pack about 1.6 steps into each loop with 4 loops and 2.4 with 2. This is the direction that \citet{zhang2026recurrence} predict. In these models, the last values become decodable at the step positions only after the
training loop count: at loop 5 with 4 loops, and at loops 3--4 with 2.

\paragraph{Dense models compute late.} They compute the values from about layer 21 of 36. In 5 of the 10 dense seeds
(five at 20 layers and five at 36), no intermediate value after $x_1$ is decodable.

This is consistent with the extension results: a loop that applies the same step each time can be run for more steps, while a stack
that computes the chain in its last layers cannot. Probes show where a value can be read out, not that the model uses it.

\subsection{The learning rate decides whether the loop learns the task within 32{,}000 training steps}
\label{sec:lrsynthetic}

At $10^{-3}$, a good rate for the dense models, the 8-loop model of the learning-rate sweep
worked only at its training count of 8 loops, at no other count from 2 to 32, like the language models of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}. No intermediate value became decodable one loop per step. But it had also learned the training range much worse: 0.54, against
0.999 at $2.5\times10^{-4}$.

A supplementary experiment (S2, \lbl{pre-specified}) trained the four looped conditions named in its plan ($r = 4$, $r = 8$, the random loop count and loops = steps) for 32{,}000 training steps with three seeds. At $10^{-3}$, they did not learn
the training range in 10 of 12 runs; at $5\times10^{-4}$, they learned it in 8 of 12, and those mostly extended about as
far as the formal runs (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}). So a high learning rate mainly keeps the looped model from learning the task within the given training steps. When it learns the task, it mostly learns the same extendable algorithm. By the plan's own criteria, the learning rate changed the solution in
one of the eight combinations of condition and rate: the random loop count at $5\times10^{-4}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}).

\subsection{In most training regimes, the model's own confidence tells it when to stop}
\label{sec:stopping}

Extra loops help in deployment only if their number can be set without the answer. One option is to always run a fixed number of extra loops. This works for the 8-loop model
(Section~\ref{sec:extension}) but not for the loops = steps model, which loses its values when run longer
(Section~\ref{sec:probes}). The other option is a rule that decides when to stop for each problem.

\paragraph{The rules compared.} The frozen design specified one rule that does not use the answer, for the loops = steps model: stop when
the state changes by less than a threshold between loops. At the freeze it was implemented as a relative change below
2\% at the answer position, with at most 32 loops. A supplementary experiment (S8, \lbl{pre-specified}) was planned after
this rule's results were known, and frozen before the comparison. It compared the state rule with three other kinds of rules on the 25
checkpoints of the formal runs (five looped conditions, five seeds each). One stops when the predicted answer has not
changed for $m$ loops. One stops when the model's top probability reaches a threshold (the confidence rule). One takes a
majority vote over the answers at several loop counts. The confidence rule is an early-stopping variant of the
maximum-confidence rule of \citet{fan2025looped}; they mention this variant but do not evaluate it. All rules were fixed in the
plan, selected on one problem set and reported on another, drawn independently (Table~\ref{tab:stopping}).

\paragraph{The state rule works for fixed loop counts, not for loops = steps.} It recovers about 80\% of the gain of the
best fixed loop count at 12 steps for the models trained with 4 or 8 loops, without hurting the training range. But it
cannot stop the loops = steps model, whose state keeps changing after $d$ loops (accuracy 0.02--0.08 at every length).

\paragraph{The model's own confidence does better.} At a threshold of 0.99, it recovers 97--98\% of that gain for the
models trained with 4 or 8 loops or a random count, using fewer loops than their training count on in-range problems.
It brings the loops = steps model to 0.76, but loses 6--7\% of the in-range problems. Among the rules that stop, a
confidence rule (at 0.99, or at 0.9 for $r = 2$) is the best, or within 0.001 of it, in every condition of S8. A state rule with a lower threshold (0.5\%) is within 0.005 of the confidence rule for 4 and 8
loops and the random loop count, and for $r = 4$ slightly above it (0.816 against 0.815). But it uses 1.5 to 3 times as
many loops. The rule that waits for the prediction to stop changing often stops on a wrong answer that no longer changes, and
the votes run all 32 loops and never beat the best fixed count. We singled out the confidence rule after this
comparison. The plan's selection criterion picked a vote for three of the five conditions; with votes set aside, it
picked the confidence rule at 0.99 only for $r = 8$ and the random loop count (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}).

\paragraph{A few extra loops in training give a model that both extends and stops.} The models that extend furthest and
the models that stop well came from different training regimes. A supplementary experiment (S9, \lbl{pre-specified})
trained the loops = steps model with 0--4 random extra loops after the last step. It then keeps its values when run
longer (lowest probe accuracy over 16 loops 0.04 $\rightarrow$ 0.91) and extends about one step less (11.6 against 12.8).
With the confidence rule it reaches 0.93 on 12-step problems (0.89--0.95 in
the five seeds), while still solving 0.998 of the problems within the training range. The 0.5\% state rule is slightly
better (0.94 against 0.93, and better in all five seeds), but runs 24 loops against 14. With the frozen state rule,
whether it stops well depends on the seed (0.52 on average): the rule works in two seeds, fails in two and partly works
in the fifth. None of the three outcomes the plan anticipated covers this. Training it costs about 1.4 times the compute
per training step of the loops = steps model.

\subsection{Against the pre-specified predictions}
\label{sec:predictions}

The frozen design made five predictions (Table~\ref{tab:p1p5} in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}). \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{P1} predicted that the largest solvable length would rise from the dense model through 2, 4 and 8
  loops, and reach at least 16 steps with 8 loops. It holds in direction, but the 8-loop model reached 9 steps, not 16.
  \item \textbf{P2} predicted that dense models of equal executed depth would be at least as good as the looped models.
  It is not supported: the looped models are ahead by 1--1.6 steps.
  \item \textbf{P3} made three predictions about running a model with a loop count other than its training count.
  (a)~Fixed-loop models get much worse: half right, since they get worse with fewer loops, but the 4- and 8-loop models
  do not get worse with more. (b)~The random-loop model does not gain from more loops on chains longer than 8 steps: it
  fails, since the model gains (0.08 to 0.49 at 16 steps). (c)~The loops = steps model, run with $d$ loops, beats itself
  run with 8 loops at every length from 9 to at least 16: it holds from 10 steps on (Section~\ref{sec:extension}).
  \item \textbf{P4} predicted that the probe table of the loops = steps model would be diagonal ($x_j$ appearing at loop
  $j$; Figure~\ref{fig:probes}a) and that of the fixed-loop models would not. It is half right: the 8-loop model's table
  is diagonal too, when a value counts as appearing at the loop where it first becomes decodable. By the design's own
  wording, the loop where a value is decoded most accurately, P4 would hold.
  \item \textbf{P5} predicted far larger differences between looped and dense models of equal parameters than on text.
  It holds.
\end{itemize}
How to judge P3(c) and P4 was decided after the results. Of the supplementary predictions on this task,
S5 held one of three, S8 two of five and S9 three of four.

\paragraph{What the loop buys on a serial task.} On a task that needs serial steps, linear probes find the
chain's intermediate values in the loop's state one after another, one per loop when the model has a loop for every
step, which is consistent with step-by-step computation.
Within the training range this gives depth, but saves no compute over a dense model of the same executed depth and
costs more than a written chain of thought. Beyond the range, the loop is the only form of extra computation here that extends. It learns
to do so when its learning rate is low enough for the task to be learned within the training steps given, and, for most training regimes,
its own confidence tells it when to stop.
