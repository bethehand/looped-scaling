\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A looped, or recurrent-depth, transformer applies one block of layers several times in a row
\citep{dehghani2019universal,geiping2025scaling,zhu2025scaling}. Running a block $r$ times costs $r$ times its
compute and adds no parameters. Two benefits are expected in return. The first is \emph{parameter efficiency}: a
looped model should behave like a larger dense model, which matters wherever memory or bandwidth rather than
arithmetic limits deployment. The second is \emph{latent reasoning}: if the loop iterates on a problem, running more
loops at inference should solve harder problems without writing out a chain of thought, a form of test-time compute
that lives inside the network.

Both claims are hard to check from the literature. Studies differ in data, tokenizer, training recipe and model
family; seeds are rarely reported; and the quantity of interest, how much one more loop is worth, is usually read off
a single fitted scaling law \citep{schwethelm2026how,prairie2026parcae,chen2026moe}. Evidence for test-time scaling
comes from two directions that are seldom put side by side: large models trained with a random number of loops, which
improve on reasoning benchmarks as more loops are run at inference, up to a task-dependent saturation
\citep{geiping2025scaling}, and small models on synthetic tasks, which solve longer problems when run longer
\citep{fan2025looped,kohli2026loop,zhang2026recurrence}.

We test both claims in one study, with one architecture family and one recipe, and every plan was frozen before its
runs. On web text, a pre-registered grid of 111 runs varies model size (10M--80M looped, dense references to 160M),
which layers are looped, how often and how they are backpropagated, and extensions add size, data, re-tuned learning
rates and dense models of nearly the looped model's depth. When text left the second claim untested, we built a task that
needs serial steps, froze its design and predictions (replacing its modular arithmetic by chains of lookups in fixed
random tables for learnability before any formal run), and compared looped models with dense models of equal parameters
and of equal executed depth, with a written chain of thought and with pause tokens.

The two settings give different answers (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}, Table~\ref{tab:summary}). On text the loop buys
parameter and data efficiency, not compute efficiency: from about 40M on, a model whose middle block is looped 4 times
is worth more than its parameters, the more so with more data, and at 40M a dense model of the same size and nearly the
same depth recovers about half of that advantage; at equal training compute and a shared learning rate the dense model
wins every measured comparison, and extra loops at inference never help: depending on its training, the looped block
runs a fixed-length program, a cycle or a fixed point. On the chain task the same architecture computes the chain step by step inside the
loop. Within the training range this buys depth, which a written chain of thought buys more cheaply; beyond it only the
loop extends, and for most training regimes its own confidence tells it when to stop.

We read the difference as a matter of three conditions, which the chain task meets once the model is trained for them:
\emph{headroom} (errors come from too few serial steps, not from missing knowledge), \emph{extendable iteration} (the
loop can run longer without destroying its state) and a \emph{stopping signal}. On web text at these sizes we saw no sign
of the first. This is an interpretation of two settings, not a tested law; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} says what would
test it.
