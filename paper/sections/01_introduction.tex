\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A looped transformer, also called a recurrent-depth transformer, runs one block of layers several times in a row
\citep{dehghani2019universal,geiping2025scaling,zhu2025scaling}. Running a block $r$ times costs $r$ times its compute
but adds almost no parameters. Two benefits are expected in return. The first is \emph{parameter efficiency}: a looped
model should behave like a larger \emph{dense} model, a standard transformer that runs each of its layers once. This
matters wherever memory or bandwidth, rather than compute, limits deployment. The second is \emph{latent reasoning}: if
each pass through the loop carries the computation one step further, running more loops at inference should solve
harder problems without writing out a chain of thought. The loop would then act as test-time compute inside the network.

Both claims are hard to check from the literature. Studies differ in data, tokeniser, training recipe and model family,
and seeds are rarely reported. For the first claim, the quantity of interest, how much one more loop is worth, is
usually read off a single fitted scaling law \citep{schwethelm2026how,prairie2026parcae,chen2026moe}. For the second,
the evidence comes from two lines of work that are seldom compared. Large models trained with a random number of loops
improve on reasoning benchmarks as more loops are run, up to a task-dependent limit \citep{geiping2025scaling}. Small
models on synthetic tasks solve longer problems when run longer \citep{fan2025looped,kohli2026loop,zhang2026recurrence}.

We test both claims with one model family and one training recipe. On web text, a pre-registered grid of 111 language
models varies the model size (looped models of 10M--80M parameters, dense models up to 160M), which layers are looped,
the loop count $r$ and how gradients flow through the loop. In this grid, a dense model runs eight layers once. A looped
model runs either all eight layers $r$ times or only the middle four, with two layers before the loop and two after it
(Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}a). With the middle block looped four times, it runs 20 layers in total; most of our text
results are for this 4-loop model. Follow-up runs add a 160M looped model, more training data, re-tuned learning rates and deeper dense models
(Section~\ref{sec:grid}).

On web text at these sizes, extra loops at inference did not help (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). We therefore also
built a synthetic task that needs serial steps, and fixed its design and predictions before the main runs. Each problem
is a chain of lookups in fixed random tables, one lookup per step; we call it the chain task. On it we compare looped
models with dense models of the same size, dense models that run as many layers in total (the same \emph{executed
depth}), dense models that write out a chain of thought, and dense models given pause tokens before the answer.

Results that were planned before their runs are marked as such; all other analyses are labelled exploratory
(Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}).

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} and Table~\ref{tab:summary} summarise the results. On web text, the pre-registered
scaling-law analysis failed. Five model sizes were too few to fit the law it relied on, and none of its hypotheses about where to loop, how often and how to backpropagate held as
stated (Section~\ref{sec:verdicts}). The other text results therefore come from
direct comparisons that need no fitted law.

At equal parameters, the value of the loop depends on model size and data. At 10M--20M with up to 20 training tokens
per parameter, the 4-loop model is no better than a dense model of its own size. At about 40M--160M with 20--40 tokens
per parameter, it is as good as a dense model 1.3--1.5 times its size, and the factor grows with the number of training tokens per parameter
(Section~\ref{sec:multiplier}). Part of this advantage comes from depth alone. At 40M, a deeper dense model of the same size, with 18 layers, recovers about half of it. The deeper model's own value does not grow with data at 40M, so depth does not explain why the loop's advantage
does. With a tuned
learning rate,
the deeper model recovers more at the shortest training budget (one seed; Section~\ref{sec:depth}).

At equal training compute, the dense model wins every measured comparison at a shared learning rate; after re-tuning
the learning rates, one comparison is a tie (Section~\ref{sec:equalcompute}). At deployment, the loop saves some weight memory, but its decoding time and KV cache
grow with every layer it runs (Section~\ref{sec:cost}). Extra loops at inference never help on text.
Depending on how it is trained, the looped block learns a fixed-length program that works only at its training loop
count, a cycle that repeats every few loops, or a fixed point where the state stops changing
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

The chain task (Section~\ref{sec:serial}) gives the opposite picture. In models with at least one loop per step, linear
probes decode one more intermediate value after each loop. Within the training range, a written chain of thought solves
the task more cheaply. Beyond it, looped models run with more loops solve longer chains, while dense models of equal executed depth and models
with pause tokens do not. The chain of thought fails there too, from its first written tokens. Since longer chains here
also mean longer prompts, this may be a failure to generalise to longer inputs rather than to more steps, and only a task
with inputs of fixed length could tell. Most looped
models can also decide when to stop: stopping once the top probability passes a threshold works well without knowing the correct
answer, although we chose this rule only after comparing several (Section~\ref{sec:stopping}).

We read the difference between the two settings as follows. Extra loops help when the model's errors come from too few
serial steps, when the loop can run longer without destroying its state, and when the model has a signal for when to
stop. The chain task meets these conditions once the model is trained for them; on web text at these sizes we saw no
sign of the first. We have not tested this interpretation, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} says what would.
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} covers the limitations, the pre-registration and the released code and results.
