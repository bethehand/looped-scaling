\section{Discussion: when is recurrent depth test-time compute?}
\label{sec:discussion}

The same architecture behaves as a parameter-efficient model on text and as an extendable serial computer on the chain
task. We offer a reading of the difference. It is an interpretation of two settings, not a tested law: in this reading,
recurrent depth works as test-time compute when three conditions hold.

\paragraph{Headroom.} The model's errors must come from too few serial steps rather than from missing knowledge. On the
chain task the dense models know every table, and within the training range their errors come from too few serial
steps; one more loop is one more step. On web text at these sizes we found no sign of such errors: the per-token gain is
a diffuse shift, and extra loops never helped, at any learning rate. This condition is the hardest to measure
independently, since here it is inferred from where extra loops help; the larger gains on math and code text hint at
more headroom there, but at this size those sets are dominated by the initialisation and the data order.

\paragraph{Extendable iteration.} The loop must be able to run longer without destroying its state, and which kind of
iteration it learns depends on how it is trained: on text a middle block with a fixed loop count becomes a
fixed-length program, a whole stack trained with truncated backpropagation a periodic cycle, and a middle block with a
random loop count a fixed point; on the chain task, models
trained with as many loops as steps lose their values when run longer, and a few random extra loops in training fix
that. A fixed point is extendable but, on text, computes nothing more; a fixed-length program computes but cannot be
extended.

\paragraph{A stopping signal.} To spend extra loops where a problem needs them, the model must tell how many to run; a
fixed surplus serves some regimes (the 8-loop model) and not others (loops = steps, which loses its values when run
longer). On the chain task the model's own confidence is such a signal for most regimes, including, once trained with a few loops past the last step,
the regime that extends furthest. We did not test stopping rules on text, where extra loops did not help.

The chain task meets all three conditions once the model is trained for them; web text at these sizes showed no sign of
headroom, and its one extendable regime gained nothing from running longer. Two follow-ups we did not run would test this
reading: a serial task whose input length does not grow with the number of steps, or this task with prompts padded to
a fixed length, which would also decide whether a written chain of thought extends when the prompt does not get longer
(the decisive control for Section~\ref{sec:extension}, outside every frozen plan); and training on text that needs more
serial steps, such as mathematics and code, compared at equal compute.

\paragraph{The value of a loop is not a constant of the architecture.} Even where the loop does not iterate, it buys
something on text, and what it buys depends on size, data and the dense model it is compared with: worth at most its own
parameters at 10M--20M with up to 20 tokens per parameter, about 1.3--1.5 times as many at 40M--160M with 20--40, and at
40M about half of that is what a deeper dense model of the same size also gets. Effective-parameter laws whose loop gain
depends on the loop count but not on model size or training tokens describe the level of the larger models but not its rise with data, and the
smaller ones not at all, and the single pre-registered $\varphi$ averaged over both. Studies at one scale, or against dense
references of one depth, will undersell or oversell the loop depending on where they sit.

\paragraph{Practical guidance.} Within the range we measured:
\begin{itemize}
  \item On text, loop when parameters, not data, compute or latency, are the constraint: the advantage at equal
  parameters grows with model size and tokens per parameter, a dense model was better at equal training compute in every
  measured comparison but one (Section~\ref{sec:equalcompute}), and at deployment the loop pays its executed layers in decoding time and KV
  cache. Try a deeper dense model of the same size first: at 40M it gave about half of the advantage for half the
  training compute, and at the shortest budget, with its learning rate tuned, all of it (one seed); at 20M, at width 320,
  it was worse than the 8-layer model.
  \item Loop a middle block with full backpropagation (at similar executed depth the middle block won every
  comparison with the whole stack, Table~\ref{tab:verdicts}), start with four loops on text (at 40M--80M eight did about as well
  at about 1.75 times the training compute), and judge at the target scale: small-scale ablations undersell the loop.
  \item Tune the looped model's learning rate separately, especially at small scale and on tasks that need serial steps,
  where at a rate that suits the dense model most looped runs did not learn the chain task in the given steps.
  \item Do not expect test-time scaling on text from standard training; sweep the loop count at inference to see whether
  a model can use more loops, and probe it to see whether it computes step by step even when it cannot.
  \item If a model must decide how long to run, its confidence was the most useful stopping signal here (a rule
  highlighted after the S8 comparison, \lbl{exploratory}), and training a few loops past the last step made it work in all
  five seeds. Within the training range of our task a written chain of thought,
  supervised on every intermediate value, was the cheaper choice; its failure beyond it came from longer prompts and
  says little about inputs of fixed length.
\end{itemize}
