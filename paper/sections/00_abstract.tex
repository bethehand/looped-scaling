\begin{abstract}
A looped transformer runs the same block of layers several times, adding depth without adding parameters. Two benefits
are claimed: it behaves like a larger model, and more loops at inference solve harder problems. We test both on web
text, with a pre-registered grid of 111 language models plus follow-up runs, and on a synthetic task that needs serial
steps.

On text, the pre-registered scaling-law analysis failed: five model sizes were too few to fit it. In exploratory
comparisons with a standard (dense) model of the same size, looping the middle four of eight layers four times gives no gain at 10M--20M parameters with up to 20 training tokens per parameter, and the loop's
value rises with training data. At 40M--160M
with 20--40 tokens per parameter, the looped model matches a dense model about 1.3--1.5 times its size. At 40M, a dense model with about the same number of parameters spread over 18 layers recovers about
half of this gain; unlike the loop's gain, its gain does not grow with data. At equal training compute, the dense model
wins every measured comparison at a shared learning rate. At equal loss, the loop saves at most 19% of weight memory
but needs 2.2--2.5 times the KV cache and decoding time, and extra loops at inference never help.

On a synthetic task of chained lookups in random tables, looped models trained on 1--8 lookups solve longer chains when
run with more loops: with 16 loops, an 8-loop model goes from 0.04 to 0.83 accuracy on 12-step chains. In that model, linear probes decode one more intermediate value after each loop. Dense models of equal depth and models given pause tokens do not
solve the longer chains. A written chain of thought is perfect within the training range at under a third of the 8-loop model's
compute; beyond it, it already fails at its first tokens, likely because the prompts are longer than any seen in
training.
\end{abstract}
