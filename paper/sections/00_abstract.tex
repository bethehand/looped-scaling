\begin{abstract}
A looped transformer runs the same block of layers several times, adding depth without adding parameters. Two benefits
are claimed: it behaves like a larger model, and more loops at inference solve harder problems. We test both on web
text, with a pre-registered grid of 111 language models plus follow-up runs, and on a synthetic task that needs serial
steps.

On text, none of the four pre-registered hypotheses held as stated; five model sizes were too few to fit the
pre-registered scaling law. In exploratory comparisons with a standard (dense) model of the same size, looping the
middle four of eight layers four times gives no gain at 10M--20M parameters with up to 20 tokens per parameter.
At 40M--160M with 20--40 tokens per parameter, the looped model matches a dense model about 1.3--1.5 times its size,
and at most sizes the factor grows with data. At 40M and the grid's learning rate, a dense model of about the same size spread over 18 layers recovers about half of this gain; its own gain does not grow with data. At equal training compute and a shared learning rate, the dense model wins every measured comparison. At
equal loss, the loop saves at most 19\% of weight memory but needs 2.2--2.5 times the KV cache and decoding time. Extra
loops at inference never help on text.

On the synthetic task, chained lookups in random tables, looped models trained on 1--8 lookups solve longer chains
when run with more loops, while dense models of equal depth and models given pause tokens do not: with 16 loops, an
8-loop model goes from 0.04 to 0.83 accuracy on 12-step chains, and in that model
linear probes decode one more intermediate value after each loop. A chain of thought trained on every intermediate value is perfect within the training range at under a third of the
8-loop model's compute; beyond it, it fails from its first tokens, likely because the prompts are longer than any seen
in training.
\end{abstract}
